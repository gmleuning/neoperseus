## Preface: On the Loyal and Righteous Water Margin[^1]

The Grand Historian said, "'The Difficulties of Persuasion' and 'Solitary Indignation' were written when worthies and sages gave vent to their indignation." Seen from this, the sages and worthies of antiquity did not write unless they were indignant. To write without indignation is like shivering when one is not cold, or groaning when one is not sick. Even if one writes, what is there in it worth seeing?

*Water Margin* is a work written out of indignation. From the time the Song house grew weak, caps and shoes were worn upside down: men of great worth stood below, while the unworthy sat above, until at last barbarians stood above and the Central Plains lay beneath them. The sovereigns and ministers of that age were still like swallows and sparrows nesting in a hall, offering tribute and calling themselves subjects, willingly bending their knees to dogs and sheep! The two masters Shi and Luo lived under the Yuan, yet their hearts remained with the Song. Though born in the days of the Yuan, they were truly indignant over the affairs of the Song. Therefore, indignant at the northern captivity of the Two Emperors, they told of the great defeat of the Liao to vent their indignation; indignant at the spineless peace after the crossing to the south, they told of the destruction of Fang La to vent their indignation. Dare one ask who vented this indignation? None other than the strongmen who once gathered at the call in the water margins! It is impossible not to call them loyal and righteous; therefore, Masters Shi and Luo recorded the tale of the water margins and named their history *Loyal and Righteous*.

How did "loyalty and righteousness" come to belong to the water margins? The reason is plain to see. How did every single man of the water margins come to be loyal and righteous? What brought them to it is equally plain to see. Now, it is right and reasonable for men of lesser virtue to serve men of greater virtue, and men of lesser worth to serve men of greater worth. If men of lesser worth command others, while men of great worth are commanded by them, will they willingly submit to service without shame? That is like men of small strength binding others, and forcing men of great strength to be bound by them—will they fold their hands and submit to the ropes without protest? The tide of circumstance must inevitably drive all the men of great strength and great worth under Heaven into the water margins. Thus it is entirely fitting to say that the host of the water margins are all men of great strength and great worth, possessed of loyalty and righteousness.

Yet none matched Song Gongming in loyalty and righteousness. Looking now at the hundred and eight men, sharing merit and fault, sharing death and life, their hearts of loyalty and righteousness were just like Song Gongming's. Song Gongming alone, though his body dwelt in the water margins, kept his heart upon the imperial court; single-mindedly seeking amnesty, wholly intent on repaying his country, he braved great perils and achieved great merit, and in the end drank poison and hanged himself so that all might die together without refusal. Such fierce extremity of loyalty and righteousness was truly enough to win the hearts of the hundred and eight! Thus he could bind them in brotherhood on Mount Liang and serve as lord of the hundred and eight. At the last, in the southern campaign against Fang La, more than half of the hundred and eight died in battle.

Moreover, Zhishen passed away sitting in meditation at Liuhe, Yan Qing wept as he took leave of his master, and the two Tong brothers fell into the River-Churning Dragon's snare. It was not that Song Gongming failed to see this; he held that shrewd foresight to save oneself was merely the calculation of a petty man, and something one loyal to his sovereign and righteous to his friends could never stoop to. This is what is meant by Song Gongming, and this is why the book is called *Loyal and Righteous*.

Could this *History* have gone unwritten? Could this *History* go unread? Therefore, those who rule a state cannot afford not to read it. Once they read this *History*, "loyalty and righteousness" will no longer be in the water margins, but will all stand beside the sovereign. Worthy chief ministers cannot afford not to read it. Once they read this *History*, "loyalty and righteousness" will no longer be in the water margins, but will all stand in the imperial court. The Ministry of War holds the pivot of military and state affairs, and regional commanders hold sole charge beyond the gates—they too cannot afford not to read it. If one day they read this *History*, then "loyalty and righteousness" will no longer be in the water margins, but will all be chosen as shields and walls, hearts and vitals of the realm. Otherwise, if not in the court, not beside the sovereign, not among the shields and walls, hearts and vitals, where will they be? In the water margins. That is why this *History* was written out of indignation. As for busybodies who use it as fodder for idle chatter, or military men who borrow its stratagems, each takes what suits his own bent—where do they ever see what is called loyalty and righteousness?

Composed by Li Zhi, Zhuowu of Wenling

Written by Sun Pu of Hulin beside the Three-Lives Stone in the midsummer of the *gengxu* year

## Prologue

{{0}} To a lyric tune:

> Look where scholars hide away in groves of books—
> How many dashing, gifted Confucians are there,
> Unconcerned by hollow fame or paltry gain!
> Carving ice and snipping snow into verse,
> Laughing and talking as they gaze upon their Wu-forged blades.
> They judge former kings and later emperors,
> Dividing true from false as they hold the Central Plain,
> While seven warring heroes churn the Spring and Autumn age.
> Rise and fall are like brittle willows;
> Life and fortune resemble an empty boat.
> Countless are those who win renown,
> Countless those whose portraits hang in halls,
> And countless more who flee from fame itself.
> In a flash the new moon sinks down the long river,
> And lakes and streams turn to mulberry fields and ancient roads.
> Strange to climb a tree in search of fish,
> Like a cornered ape choosing its branch,
> Or a bird wounded by the bow shying at any curved wood.
> Better instead to drain the cup in your palm,
> And listen once more to a fresh-sung air.

A poem says:

> Amid the tangled strife of the Five Dynasties,
> One morning the clouds parted and Heaven was seen again.
> Grass and trees for a hundred years knew fresh rain and dew;
> Carts and script across ten thousand *li* united old rivers and hills.
> Common lanes and alleys were dressed in silk and gauze;
> From many towers and terraces sounded pipes and strings.
> The people rejoiced in days of Great Peace free of trouble,
> Sleeping late into the morning amid endless orioles and blossoms.

The story goes that these eight lines of verse were composed by a famous Confucian scholar at the court of the late Emperor Shenzong of Song, surnamed Shao, with the given name Yaofu and the Daoist style Master Kangjie. He wrote them in lament over the ceaseless clash of shields and spears under Heaven during the Five Dynasties and the waning Tang. In those days, the realm belonged to Liang in the morning and to Jin by evening—truly as the saying goes: "Zhu, Li, Shi, Liu, and Guo; Liang, Tang, Jin, Han, and Zhou: fifteen emperors in all, sowing disorder for fifty autumns." Later, moved by the turning cycle of Heaven's Way, the Martial and Virtuous Emperor Taizu was born in the Armored Horse Barracks. When this sage of the age came into the world, red light filled the sky and a rare fragrance lingered overnight without fading, for he was the Great Thunderclap Immortal of the Upper Realm descended to earth. Heroic and fierce, broad-minded and generous in wisdom, no emperor since antiquity could match this Son of Heaven. With a single staff as tall as himself, he beat four hundred military prefectures into bearing the surname Zhao. That Son of Heaven swept clean the universe, pacified the Central Plain, named his dynasty Great Song, and established his capital at Bianliang. He was the leader of nine reigns and eight emperors, the founding sovereign of a four-hundred-year dynasty. That is why Master Shao Yaofu sang in praise: "One morning the clouds parted and Heaven was seen again"—just as if the common people had beheld the face of sun and sky once more.

Not only did this master chant his praise; at that time on Mount Hua, the Western Peak, there lived a recluse named Chen Tuan, a man lofty in the Way and rich in virtue who could read the signs in wind and cloud. One day he rode his donkey down the mountain, and as he was traveling along the Huayin road, he overheard travelers on the way spreading the news: "Today in the Eastern Capital, Emperor Shizong of Zhou has yielded the throne to Inspector Zhao, who has ascended as emperor." When Master Chen Tuan heard this, joy filled his heart; raising his hand to his forehead, he burst out laughing on his donkey's back and tumbled right off the beast. People asked him the reason. The master said, "From this day on, the world is settled!" It answered to the heart of Heaven above, the lie of the earth below, and the harmony of mankind between.

Having accepted the abdication in the *gengshen* year, he founded the dynasty and mounted the throne, reigning for seventeen years, and Great Peace under Heaven was settled from that time. He passed the throne to his imperial younger brother, who ascended as Taizong. Emperor Taizong reigned for twenty-two years and passed the throne to the Crown Prince. The emperor of that reign was the Barefoot Great Immortal of the Upper Realm. When he was born, he wept and wailed day and night without stopping. The court posted a yellow proclamation summoning anyone who could cure him. This moved the Court of Heaven, which dispatched the Great White Planet Venus down to the mortal world; taking the form of an old man, he came forward and tore down the yellow proclamation, claiming he could cure the Crown Prince's crying. The officials guarding the proclamation led him to the foot of the hall to have audience with Emperor Zhenzong, whose imperial decree bade him enter the inner gardens to see the Crown Prince. The old man went straight into the palace, took the Crown Prince in his arms, and whispered eight words in his ear, whereupon the Crown Prince stopped crying. Without giving his name, the old man turned into a gust of clear breeze and vanished. What were the eight words he spoke into the child's ear? He said: "For civil affairs there is the Literary Star; for martial affairs, the Military Star." In truth, the Jade Emperor had dispatched two stars from the Purple Tenuity Palace down to earth to assist the emperor of this reign. The Literary Star was Bao Zheng, Prefect of the Southern Magistracy of Kaifeng and Grand Academician of the Dragon Diagram Hall; the Military Star was Di Qing, Grand Marshal of the Expedition against Western Xia.

These two worthy ministers came forth to assist this emperor, whose temple name was Emperor Renzong. He reigned for forty-two years and changed his reign title nine times. From his accession in the *guihai* year, the first year of Tiansheng, through the ninth year of Tiansheng, Great Peace prevailed under Heaven, the five grains yielded abundant harvests, the myriad people rejoiced in their livelihoods, no one pocketed lost goods on the road, and doors were left unbolted at night; those nine years were called the First Harvest. From the first year of Mingdao through the third year of Huangyou, those nine years were also plentiful and were called the Second Harvest. From the fourth year of Huangyou through the second year of Jiayou, the crops in the fields ripened abundantly for nine years, and this was called the Third Harvest. Three times nine—twenty-seven consecutive years—were known as the Era of the Three Harvests. At that time the common people enjoyed some happiness; who would have thought that extreme joy begets sorrow? In the early spring of the third year of Jiayou, a pestilence raged throughout the realm. From south of the Yangtze all the way to the Two Capitals, not a single place escaped the affliction. From every prefecture and commandery under Heaven, memorials poured into the capital like snowflakes.

To speak now of the Eastern Capital, inside and outside the city walls, more than half the soldiers and civilians died. Academician Bao, Prefect of Kaifeng, personally took the prescriptions of the Bureau for Benefiting the People and Harmonizing Medicines, spent his own salary to compound drugs, and tried to save the myriad people, yet there was no stopping the disease—the plague only grew fiercer. All the civil and military officials deliberated and gathered in the Waiting-for-the-Clepsydra Hall to await the morning audience and memorialize the Son of Heaven, bent on offering prayers and sacrifices to avert the pestilence. Had it not been for this affair, how would the thirty-six Heavenly Spirits have descended to the mortal world, and the seventy-two Earthly Fiends come down among men, shaking the universe of the Song state and throwing the altars of the Zhao house into turmoil? A poem bears witness to this:

A poem says:

> The myriad surnames lived in harmony amid nourishing grace,
> Endless the joy in that Era of the Three Harvests.
> Who knew a reign of rites, music, pipes, and bells
> Would turn into a thicket of shields, spears, swords, and halberds?
> Within the Water Margin stronghold camped chivalrous men;
> Inside Liangshan Marsh gathered heroes.
> Carefully trace the numbers of order, chaos, rise, and fall—
> All belong to the workings of Yin and Yang and Creation's craft.

## Chapter 1: Master Zhang Prays Away a Plague; Marshal Hong Lets Loose the Demons

{{1}} A poem says:

> In crimson cap the rooster-man calls the watches of dawn;
> The Keeper of Robes now presents the kingfisher-cloud fur.
> The gates of Ninefold Heaven open the palace halls;
> Caps and gowns from ten thousand states bow before the beaded crown.
> Sunlight just touches the Immortal's Palm and sets it stirring;
> Incense smoke drifts beside the imperial dragon robes.
> Audience ended, five-colored edicts must be drafted;
> Jade pendants chime back to the banks of the Phoenix Pool.

The story goes that during the reign of Emperor Renzong of Great Song, at the third watch-beat of the fifth watch on the third day of the third month of the third year of Jiayou, the Son of Heaven ascended his throne in the Purple Mysterium Hall to receive the morning homage of all his officials. Look at that scene:

> Auspicious clouds shroud the Phoenix Pavilion; lucky vapors cloak the Dragon Tower.
> Mist-veiled imperial willows brush the banners and flags; dew-laden palace flowers greet the swords and halberds.
> In the shadow of celestial incense, jade hairpins and pearled shoes gather on the vermilion courtyard; amid strains of fairy music, embroidered jackets and brocade robes attend the imperial carriage.
> Pearl blinds roll up, revealing the golden palanquin in the Hall of Gold; phoenix-tail fans part, halting the jeweled litter before white jade steps.
> Faintly three cracks of the silence-whip ring out; tier upon tier, civil and military ranks stand aligned.

Thereupon the Master of Ceremonies cried out, "Those with business step forward from the ranks and memorialize early; if there is none, roll down the blinds and dismiss the court." Out from the massed ranks stepped Prime Minister Zhao Zhe and Assistant Councilor Wen Yanbo, who bowed and memorialized: "At present a pestilence rages in the capital, leaving the people with no means to live and killing soldiers and civilians in great numbers. We humbly beg Your Majesty to remit crimes in your broad mercy, lighten punishments, and reduce taxes, so as to avert Heaven's calamity and rescue the myriad people." Hearing their memorial, the Son of Heaven urgently commanded the Hanlin Academy to draft an edict at once: on one hand granting general amnesty to prisoners throughout the realm and remitting all taxes and levies on the common people; on the other ordering all Daoist abbeys and Buddhist monasteries in the capital to perform meritorious services to ward off the disaster. Unexpectedly, the plague grew even worse that year. Learning of this, Emperor Renzong felt uneasy in his dragon person and summoned all his officials once more. As they deliberated together, one great minister stepped forward from the ranks to memorialize. Looking closely, the Son of Heaven saw it was Assistant Councilor Fan Zhongyan. Having performed his obeisance, he memorialized: "At present Heaven's calamity rages; soldiers and civilians are plunged in mire and charcoal, unable to survive from morning to evening, and people meet the doom of death. In your servant's humble opinion, to avert this disaster, Your Majesty should summon the Hereditary Celestial Master of Han to travel day and night to court and hold a Grand Offering of All-Embracing Heaven with thirty-six hundred portions in the Forbidden Precincts of the capital, memorializing the Supreme Emperor on High; thus may the pestilence among the people be averted." Emperor Renzong approved the memorial. He urgently ordered a Hanlin Academician to draft an edict, wrote it out in his own imperial brush, bestowed a stick of imperial incense, and commissioned Hong Xin, Marshal of the Palace Guard and Inspector Within and Without, as Imperial Envoy to travel to Mount Longhu in Xinzhou, Jiangxi, and invite the Hereditary Celestial Master, Perfected Man Zhang, to travel day and night to court to pray away the plague. Right there in the Golden Hall he lit the imperial incense and personally handed the vermilion edict to Marshal Hong as his envoy, bidding him set out at once.

Receiving the imperial decree, Marshal Hong took leave of the Son of Heaven and dared not linger. With attendants carrying the edict on their backs and the imperial incense in a golden casket, taking several dozen men, he mounted post-horses, and with his whole retinue left the Eastern Capital and took the road straight toward Guixi County in Xinzhou. Along the road, look at what they saw:

> Distant hills piled in kingfisher green; far waters clear and pure.
> Rare flowers burst open, spreading brocade across the woods; tender willows dance, brushing the ground with golden threads.
> Gentle breezes and warm sun as they pass country inns and mountain villages; straight roads and level sands where they lodge by night at post-houses and relay stations.
> Silk robes billow amid the red dust; fine steeds gallop along the purple highways.

To tell of Marshal Hong bearing the imperial vermilion edict with his retinue along the road: lodging by night at post-houses, traveling by day from relay station to relay station, greeted from afar and met close at hand, drinking when thirsty and eating when hungry, after more than a single day they arrived in Xinzhou, Jiangxi. Officials great and small came out beyond the outer wall to welcome him, and at once sent word to the abbot and Daoists of Upper Clarity Palace on Mount Longhu to prepare to receive the edict. The next day, all the officials escorted the Marshal to the foot of Mount Longhu. There, a great host of Daoists from Upper Clarity Palace, ringing bells and beating drums, bearing incense, flowers, lanterns, candles, banners, streamers, and jeweled canopies to a full ensemble of celestial music, came down the mountain to welcome the vermilion edict, escorting it all the way to the front of Upper Clarity Palace, where they dismounted. When the Marshal looked upon that palace, it was truly a magnificent Upper Clarity Palace! Look at it:

> Green pines twist and coil; emerald cypresses cast deep shade.
> Over the gate hangs an imperial plaque in gold script; along the doors stand spirit talismans in jade seal-characters.
> Beside the Altar of the Void Emperor, weeping willows and prized blooms half-appear; around the Elixir-Refining Furnace, hoary pines and ancient junipers cast dappled shade.
> Along the left wall, Heavenly Warriors and Mighty Men attend the Perfected Lord of Great Unity; along the right, Jade Maidens and Golden Lads cluster around the Great Emperor of Purple Tenuity.
> With unbound hair and drawn sword, the Dark Warrior of the North treads tortoise and serpent; in felt slippers and lofty cap, the Old Man of the South Pole subdues dragon and tiger.
> In front stand ranked the Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions; behind are arrayed the Thirty-Two Heavenly Emperors.
> Below the stone steps clear water murmurs; behind the compound walls fine mountains encircle all.
> Cranes wear cinnabar crowns; tortoises grow green moss.
> In treetops gray gibbons offer fruit; among sedge grass white deer carry magic mushrooms in their mouths.
> In the Hall of the Three Pure Ones, Daoists chant pacing the void to the chime of golden bells; before the Shrine of the Four Saints, perfected masters bow to the Dipper to the strike of jade chimes.
> On the incense-offering terrace, rosy clouds flash upon green glaze; at the general-summoning altar, the red sun sways on red agate.
> At dawn auspicious clouds appeared outside the gate—one might think the Celestial Master was seeing off Laozi.

At once, from the abbot perfected master down to the Daoist novices and attendants, welcoming before and guiding behind, they ushered him into the Hall of the Three Pure Ones and respectfully placed the edict in the center for veneration. Marshal Hong asked the supervising master, "Where is the Celestial Master at present?" The abbot stepped forward and replied, "Let me inform Your Lordship: the ancestral master of this generation, styled Celestial Master Xujing, is by nature fond of lofty purity and weary of welcoming and seeing off guests. He has built himself a thatched hermitage on the very summit of Mount Longhu, where he cultivates perfection and nourishes his nature, and therefore does not reside in this palace." The Marshal said, "Now that the Son of Heaven summons him by edict, how am I to see him?" The perfected master replied, "Allow me to explain: the imperial edict is enshrined for the time being in the hall, and we poor Daoists dare not open and read it. First let Your Lordship come to the abbot's quarters for tea, and then we may deliberate further."

Thereupon they left the vermilion edict enshrined in the Hall of the Three Pure Ones and went with all the officials to the abbot's quarters. The Marshal took the central seat; stewards served tea and then brought out vegetarian fare, with delicacies of land and water in full array. After the meal, the Marshal again asked the perfected master, "Since the Celestial Master is in his hermitage on the summit, why not send someone to invite him down to meet me and open and proclaim the vermilion edict?" The perfected master replied, "Your Lordship, although the ancestral master of this generation is on the summit, his attainment in the Way is extraordinary—lofty, pure, free, and weary of earthly dust. He can ride the mist and raise the clouds, with no fixed abode, and has never come down the mountain. Even we poor Daoists rarely get to see him in ordinary times. How could anyone invite him down?" The Marshal said, "If that is so, how can I see him? At this moment pestilence rages in the capital, and the reigning Son of Heaven has specially dispatched me as his envoy, bearing the imperial vermilion edict and personally carrying dragon incense to invite the Celestial Master to perform a Grand Offering of All-Embracing Heaven with thirty-six hundred portions, so as to avert Heaven's calamity and save the myriad people. What is to be done in such a case?" The perfected master replied, "If the court and Son of Heaven wish to save the myriad people, the only way is for Your Lordship to summon up a heart of utmost sincerity, fast and bathe, change into plain cloth garments, leave all attendants behind, carry the edict on your own back, burn the imperial incense, and walk up the mountain on foot, bowing in reverence to entreat the Celestial Master—only then may you be permitted to see him. If your heart lacks utmost sincerity, you will make the trip in vain and still fail to see him." Hearing this, the Marshal said, "I have kept a vegetarian diet all the way from the capital to this place—how could my heart lack utmost sincerity? Since that is how it is, I will do as you say and go up the mountain at first light tomorrow." That night each retired to rest.

At the fifth watch the next morning, the Daoists rose, prepared fragrant bathwater and vegetarian food, and invited the Marshal to rise. He bathed in the fragrant water, changed into a fresh suit of plain cloth clothes, put hemp sandals and straw shoes on his feet, ate the vegetarian meal, took the vermilion edict wrapped in yellow silk and strapped it onto his back, and carried in his hand a silver censer in which the imperial incense burned steadily. A host of Daoists escorted him to the rear of the mountain and pointed out the path. The perfected master added, "Your Lordship wishes to save the myriad people; let no thought of turning back or regret arise in your heart, only press upward with utmost sincerity." Taking leave of the crowd and reciting the precious title of the Celestial Worthy, the Marshal strode up the mountain. Approaching halfway up, he looked up and saw the great peak piercing the very Milky Way—truly a mighty mountain! Indeed:

> Its roots coil around the corners of the earth; its summit touches the heart of Heaven.
> Seen from afar, it grinds away the tracks of scattered clouds; viewed up close, it swallows whole the bright moon's soul. Uneven heights are called mountains; rocky side-passages are called defiles;
> Steep lone ridges are called paths; the flat expanse atop is called the summit; round peaks with massive bases are called crests; lairs hiding tigers and leopards are called dens;
> Crags sheltering wind and cloud are called cliffs; caves where lofty recluses dwell are called grottos; bounded realms are called domains; trails where woodcutters come and go are called tracks;
> Ways wide enough for carts and horses are called roads; rushing water with voice is called a torrent; headwaters of ancient fords are called streams; water dripping from cliff ledges is called a spring.
> The left wall serves as screen; the right wall serves as reflection. What issues forth is cloud; what gathers in is mist.
> Awl-sharp peaks look slender; jagged precipices look sheer; overhanging ledges look perilous; sheer-hewn faces look flat.
> A thousand peaks vie in beauty; ten thousand ravines contend in flow. Waterfalls fly slantwise; vines and creepers hang upside down.
> When tigers roar, wind rises at the valley mouth; when gibbons cry, the moon sinks at the mountain's waist.
> It is just like a thousand pieces of jade dyed in indigo, or ten thousand drifts of mist veiled in kingfisher gauze.

Marshal Hong walked alone for a time along winding slopes and twisting paths, grasping creepers and climbing vines. After crossing several ridges and covering more than three to five *li*, his feet grew sore and his legs weak until he could barely move. Saying nothing aloud, he wavered inwardly, thinking to himself: "I am a noble official and son of an eminent house at court; in the capital I slept on double mattresses and ate from rows of tripods, yet still felt weary. When did I ever wear straw sandals to walk mountain trails like this? Who knows where that Celestial Master is, yet I am made to suffer such hardship!" Before he had gone another thirty or fifty paces, shoulders hunched and gasping for breath, a gust of wind rose from a mountain hollow; as the wind passed, from behind a pine tree came a roar like rolling thunder, and out leaped a huge golden-striped tiger with slanting eyes and white forehead! Startled out of his wits, Marshal Hong cried, "Aiya!" and tumbled backward onto the ground. Stealing a glance at the tiger, look at what he saw:

> Its fur is draped in a swath of golden yellow; its paws bare eighteen silver hooks.
> Eyes like lightning and tail like a whip; mouth like a blood-bowl and fangs like halberds.
> Stretching its loins and spreading its forelegs, its bearing is ferocious; lashing its tail and shaking its head, its voice is a thunderbolt.
> Every fox and hare in the hills hides away; every roebuck and deer down by the stream conceals its tracks.

The tiger circled Marshal Hong to left and right, roared a while, and then bounded off down the rear slope of the mountain. Lying beneath a pine root, Marshal Hong was so terrified that his thirty-six teeth chattered together in pairs, his heart pounded up and down like fifteen buckets in a well, his whole body felt numb as if struck by heavy palsy, and his two legs trembled like a defeated fighting cock, while his mouth groaned endlessly in misery. Only after the tiger had been gone the time it takes to drink a cup of tea did he manage to scramble up, gather the censer from the ground, relight the dragon incense, and resume climbing the mountain, determined to find the Celestial Master. He had gone no more than thirty or fifty paces further, sighing heavily and complaining, "The Emperor set an imperial deadline and sent me here to suffer such fright and terror!" Hardly had he spoken when another gust of wind blew up, wafting a foul, venomous stench straight at him. Fixing his eyes on the spot, he heard a rustling among the bamboo and vines by the mountainside, and out darted a snake as thick as a water bucket and white as falling snow! Seeing it, the Marshal took another fright, dropped the hand-censer, cried out, "This time I am dead for sure!" and fell backward beside a boulder. Cracked-eyed, he peered at the snake; look at what he saw:

> Raising its head, a fierce gale rises; flashing its eyes, lightning bolts spring forth.
> When it writhes, gorges split and ridges topple; when it breathes, clouds billow and mists spew.
> Its scales and plates scatter like a thousand chips of jade; its tail-tip coils slantwise like a heap of silver.

The great snake darted straight to the boulder, coiled itself in a heap facing Marshal Hong, shot golden light from its two eyes, opened its huge jaws, flicked out its tongue, and blew its venomous breath right into Marshal Hong's face, scaring his three *hun* souls into scattering and his seven *po* spirits into floating away. After staring at Marshal Hong for a time, the snake slithered off down the mountain and was gone in an instant. Only then did the Marshal manage to crawl up, saying, "Mercy! That scared me to death!" Looking at his skin, his goosebumps were as big as fried dough dumplings. He cursed the Daoists under his breath: "Intolerable insolence, making sport of me! Making me suffer such terror! If I don't find the Celestial Master up on the mountain, I'll have plenty to say to them when I get back down!" Picking up the silver censer again and straightening his edict, clothes, and cap, he was just about to resume his climb. As he was about to step forward, he heard the faint sound of a flute drifting from behind the pines, drawing steadily closer. Gazing intently, the Marshal saw a Daoist boy riding backward on a yellow ox, playing a transverse iron flute as he came around the mountain hollow. As the Marshal looked at the boy, look at what he saw:

> Two topknots tied upon his head; a single robe of azure cloth upon his body.
> Around his waist a sash braided of grass; upon his feet hemp-and-straw sandals.
> Bright eyes and white teeth, airy and untainted by earthly dust; dark locks and rosy cheeks, pure and wholly free of vulgar air.

In days of old, Lü Dongbin wrote a poem on a herdboy that put it well:

> Grass spreads across the open wilds for six or seven *li*;
> A flute teases the evening breeze with three or four notes.
> Returning after a full meal past dusk,
> Without doffing his coir raincoat, he lies down in the bright moon.

There was that Daoist boy, smiling gently as he rode his yellow ox, playing his transverse iron flute as he came across the ridge. Seeing him, Marshal Hong called out, "Where do you come from? Do you recognize me?" The Daoist boy paid no heed and kept on playing his flute. After the Marshal had called out several times, the boy laughed heartily, pointed his iron flute at Marshal Hong, and said, "You have come here—could it be you wish to see the Celestial Master?" Greatly startled, the Marshal said, "You are a herdboy—how do you know that?" The boy smiled and said, "Early this morning I was attending the Celestial Master in his thatched hermitage, and I heard him say: 'The reigning Emperor Renzong at court has dispatched a Marshal Hong, bearing a vermilion edict and imperial incense, to come to this mountain and summon me to the Eastern Capital to perform a Grand Offering of All-Embracing Heaven of thirty-six hundred portions to pray away the pestilence under Heaven. I am now going off on a crane and riding the clouds.' By this hour he must already be gone and is no longer in the hermitage. Do not go up. The mountain is full of venomous reptiles and fierce beasts, and I fear they may harm your life." The Marshal asked again, "Do not tell me lies." Giving a single laugh without answering, the Daoist boy played his iron flute again and rode around the mountain slope out of sight. The Marshal pondered: "How could this youngster know every detail of the matter? The Celestial Master must have instructed him. It is settled for certain." He thought of climbing further, yet he had just been frightened half to death and had nearly lost his life; better to go back down the mountain.

Holding the censer, the Marshal retraced his path and hurried down the mountain. The Daoists met him and invited him into the abbot's quarters to sit down. The perfected master asked the Marshal, "Did you see the Celestial Master?" The Marshal said, "I am a noble official at court—how could you make me walk such mountain trails and suffer such hardship that I nearly lost my life? First, halfway up the mountain, out leaped a huge tiger with slanting eyes and white forehead, scaring my souls and spirits right out of me. Before I had gone past another ridge, a snow-white giant snake darted out from the bamboo and vines, coiled into a heap, and blocked my path. Had my good fortune not been great, how could I ever have returned to the capital alive? All of this was you Daoists making sport of me!" The perfected master replied, "How would we poor Daoists dare slight a great minister? That was the ancestral master testing Your Lordship's heart. Although there are snakes and tigers on this mountain, they never harm people." The Marshal added, "Just when I could walk no further and was about to climb another slope, out from behind the pines came a Daoist boy riding a yellow ox and playing an iron flute, coming right across the mountain. I asked him, 'Where are you from? Do you know me?' He said, 'I already know everything,' and told me: 'The Celestial Master left word that he mounted a crane and rode the clouds toward the Eastern Capital early this morning.' Therefore I came back." The perfected master said, "What a pity Your Lordship missed him! That herdboy was none other than the Celestial Master himself." The Marshal said, "If he is the Celestial Master, why was he dressed so shabbily?" The perfected master replied, "The Celestial Master of this generation is no ordinary person! Though young in years, his attainment in the Way is extraordinary. He is a being beyond bounds who manifests himself in all four quarters with utmost efficacy. All the world calls him Ancestral Master Daotong." Marshal Hong said, "How blind I was, failing to recognize the true Master when face to face, and missing him right before my eyes!" The perfected master said, "Your Lordship may set your heart at ease. Since the ancestral master gave his dharma command and said he had gone, by the time Your Lordship returns to the capital, this Grand Offering will already be completed by the ancestral master." Hearing this, the Marshal finally felt relieved. The perfected master ordered a banquet prepared to entertain the Marshal, had the vermilion edict respectfully stored inside an imperial-script casket to be kept in Upper Clarity Palace, and burned the dragon incense in the Hall of the Three Pure Ones. That day a grand vegetarian feast and banquet were laid out in the abbot's quarters; they drank until nightfall, when the banquet ended, and lodged there until dawn.

After breakfast the next morning, the perfected master, the Daoists, and the supervising stewards invited the Marshal to tour the mountain. The Marshal was delighted. Followed by many attendants, he walked out of the abbot's quarters with two Daoist boys leading the way, strolling in front of and behind the palace to admire the many sights. In the Hall of the Three Pure Ones, the splendor was beyond words. Along the left gallery were the Hall of Nine Heavens, the Hall of Purple Tenuity, and the Hall of the North Pole; along the right gallery were the Hall of Great Unity, the Hall of the Three Officials, and the Hall of Exorcising Evil. Having viewed all these halls, they came to a place behind the right gallery, where Marshal Hong saw a separate hall standing apart. All around it ran a wall of pounded pepper-red clay; in front were two leaves of vermilion lattice doors, locked with a padlock as thick as a man's arm, and pasted crisscross over them were more than ten sealing strips, stamped over and over with vermilion seals. Beneath the eaves hung a vermilion-lacquered plaque inscribed with four golden characters reading: "Hall of Subduing Demons." Pointing at the door, the Marshal asked, "What place is this hall?" The perfected master answered, "This is the hall where the ancient Ancestral Celestial Master locked away and suppressed the Demon Kings." The Marshal asked again, "Why are so many sealing strips pasted over and over upon it?" The perfected master replied, "This was where our founding ancestor, National Preceptor Dongxuan of the Great Tang, sealed up the Demon Kings. Each succeeding generation of Celestial Master has with his own hand added another sealing strip, so that his descendants dare not recklessly open it and let loose the Demon Lords, which would bring terrible calamity. Through eight or nine generations of ancestral masters now, they have sworn never to open it. The lock is cast solid with molten bronze—who knows what is inside? I, your humble Daoist, have served as abbot of this palace for over thirty years and have only heard tell of it." Hearing this, Marshal Hong felt startled and curious, thinking to himself, "Let me just take a look at what a Demon King looks like." He said to the perfected master, "Open the door for me; I want to see what the Demon Kings look like." The perfected master pleaded, "Your Lordship, we dare not open this hall on any account. The founding Ancestral Celestial Master left strict warning: 'Hereafter no one is permitted to open it on his own authority.'" The Marshal laughed and said, "Nonsense! You people want to conjure up weird tales to delude the good common folk, deliberately setting up a place like this and pretending you have locked up Demon Kings in order to show off your Daoist arts. I have read books of every sort, yet when did I ever see a method for locking up demons? The realm of gods and ghosts lies separated in the nether dark—I do not believe there are Demon Kings inside! Open it for me at once, and let me see what these Demon Kings are like." Three times and five times the perfected master implored, "This hall must not be opened, lest it provoke calamity and bring harm to people." Flying into a rage, the Marshal pointed at the Daoists and said, "If you do not open it for me to see, when I return to court I will first memorialize the crime of you Daoists obstructing the imperial edict, disobeying the sacred will, and preventing me from seeing the Celestial Master; then I will memorialize that you privately established this hall, falsely claiming to lock up Demon Kings to delude soldiers and civilians. I will have all your ordination certificates revoked and have you tattooed and exiled to distant, malignant military prefectures to suffer!" Fearing Marshal Hong's power and authority, the perfected master and his fellows had no choice but to call several stoker-Daoists, first peel off the sealing strips, and smash open the great lock with an iron hammer. Pushing open the doors and looking inside, they saw pitch darkness; look at it:

> Dim and silent, shadowy and obscure; for hundreds of years untouched by sunlight, for myriad ages never seeing the bright moon's shadow.
> North cannot be told from south, nor east from west. Thick black smoke chills whoever approaches; dank cold air pierces the body and makes it shiver.
> A place untrodden by human feet, a haunt where sprites and goblins come and go.
> Opening both eyes wide is like being blind; stretching out both hands, one cannot see one's palm.
> Forever like the thirtieth night of the month, yet eerie as the fifth watch before dawn.

All entered the hall together, where it was so pitch-dark nothing could be seen. The Marshal ordered his attendants to fetch a dozen or more torches, light them, and bring them in. When they shone the light around, there was nothing else on any side—only a stone stele standing in the very center, about five or six feet tall, resting on a large stone tortoise base more than half sunk in the mud. Shining the torches on the front of the stele, they saw it covered with dragon-script and phoenix-seal characters, celestial writing and talismanic registers that no one could read. Shining the torches on the back of the stele, however, there were four large characters in regular script chiseled into the stone: "Open upon meeting Hong." Was it not that, first, the Heavenly Spirit stars were fated to enter the world; second, the Song dynasty was destined to manifest loyal and good men; and third, by coincidence it met with Hong Xin? Was this not Heaven's decree? Seeing these four characters, Marshal Hong was overjoyed and said to the perfected master, "You tried to stop me, yet how comes it that hundreds of years ago my surname was already inscribed here? 'Open upon meeting Hong'—plainly it bids me open and look! What harm can it do me? Those Demon Kings must all be beneath this stone stele. You attendants, call more of these stoker-Daoists for me, and bring hoes and iron spades to dig it up!" The perfected master hurriedly remonstrated, "Your Lordship! You must not dig! Lest it bring calamity and injure people—it is far from safe!" Furious, the Marshal roared, "What do you Daoists understand? The stele clearly says it was waiting to meet me to be opened—how dare you obstruct me? Hurry and call men to dig!" Three times and five times more the perfected master pleaded, "I fear ill will come of it." How would the Marshal listen? They had no choice but to gather everyone, first topple the stone stele, and heave together to dig out the stone tortoise. It took half a day just to pry it up; digging down further, about three or four feet deep, they uncovered a huge slab of bluestone roughly ten feet square. Marshal Hong ordered them to pry that up as well. Again the perfected master pleaded bitterly, "Do not dig it up!" The Marshal would not hear of it. Everyone had to heave the stone slab up together. Looking down, beneath the slab lay a subterranean pit ten thousand fathoms deep. From within the pit came a deafening *kua-la-la* roar—no ordinary sound, just as if:

> Heaven collapsed and earth caved in; sacred peaks shook and mountains crumbled.
> On the Qiantang River, the tidal bore surges out through the Sea Gate; atop Mount Taihua, the Giant Spirit God splits a peak in two with one cleave.
> Gonggong in his fury cast off his helmet and butted down Mount Buzhou; the Strongman wielded his might and hurled his mace to smash the First Emperor's carriage.
> A single gale snaps a thousand stalks of bamboo; midnight thunder crashes amid an army of a hundred thousand.

As that roar echoed away, a streak of black vapor rolled up out of the pit, tearing off half a corner of the hall's roof. That black vapor shot straight up into mid-heaven, scattered in the sky into a hundred or more streaks of golden light, and flew off in all four directions. Startled out of their wits, everyone let out a shout and fled, throwing down hoes and spades and stampeding out of the hall, knocking each other down and tumbling head over heels without number. Marshal Hong stood gaping in stupefaction, at a total loss, his face the color of earth. Rushing out under the gallery, he saw the perfected master come forward crying woe without end. The Marshal asked, "What demons were those that escaped?" In no more than a few sentences, in less than a single speech, the perfected master explained the cause. And thereby it was destined that an emperor of that dynasty would find no peace on his pillow by night and forget his meals by day; straightway fierce tigers would lurk in Wanzicheng, and flying dragons gather in Liao'erwa.

## Chapter 2: Instructor Wang Slips Away to Yan'an Prefecture; Nine-Dragon Raises Havoc in Shi Family Village

{{2}} A poem says:

> The dark lock of ages is opened in a single dawn;
> Heavenly Spirits and Earthly Fiends emerge from the nether terrace.
> From nothing, needless trouble has always bred more trouble;
> Meant to avert disaster, the act invites disaster instead.
> The altars of state from this day swirl in clouds and turmoil;
> Weapons and war raise clamor everywhere.
> Though Gao Qiu's treachery is hateful indeed,
> Hong Xin from this hour sows the womb of calamity.

The story goes that the abbot perfected master said to Marshal Hong, "Your Lordship does not know: originally in this hall, the ancient Ancestral Celestial Master, Perfected Man Dongxuan, handed down his dharma talisman and left word: 'Within this hall are locked thirty-six Heavenly Spirit stars and seventy-two Earthly Fiend stars—one hundred and eight Demon Lords in all. Above them is erected a stone stele engraved with dragon-script and phoenix-seal celestial talismans to suppress them here. Should they ever be released into the world, they will surely torment all living creatures below.' Now Your Lordship has let them escape—what is to be done? In days to come they will surely prove a future calamity!" Hearing this, Marshal Hong broke out in a cold sweat from head to toe and could not stop trembling. Hastily packing his baggage and gathering his attendants, he went down the mountain and returned to the capital. Having seen the official off, the perfected master and Daoists returned to the palace, repaired the hall, and set the stone stele upright once more, of which no more need be said.

Meanwhile, on the road back, Marshal Hong instructed his attendants to say nothing to outsiders about letting the demons escape, lest the Son of Heaven learn of it and blame him. Nothing of note happened on the journey, and traveling day and night they returned to the capital. Entering Bianliang, they heard people saying that the Celestial Master had performed meritorious rites for seven days and nights in the Forbidden Precincts of the Eastern Capital, bestowing talismans far and wide to avert the pestilence, so that the plague had completely vanished and soldiers and civilians were at peace; the Celestial Master had then taken leave of the court and returned to Mount Longhu on his crane and clouds. At morning audience the next day, Marshal Hong saw the Son of Heaven and memorialized: "The Celestial Master rode his crane and clouds and reached the capital ahead of us; your servant and his escort traveled by post stages and only just arrived." Renzong approved his memorial, rewarded Hong Xin, and restored him to his former post, of which no more need be said.

Later, after reigning for forty-two years in all, Emperor Renzong passed away without a crown prince, and the throne passed to the son of Yunrang, Prince An'yi of Pu—a grandson of Emperor Taizu—who took the imperial title Yingzong. After reigning four years, he passed the throne to the Crown Prince, Emperor Shenzong, who reigned eighteen years and passed the throne to the Crown Prince, Emperor Zhezong. At that time Great Peace reigned throughout the world and all four quarters were tranquil.

Now in Bianliang, Kaifeng Prefecture, the Eastern Capital, in the Xuanwu Guard, there was a dissolute wastrel from a ruined family, surnamed Gao and second-born among his brothers. From childhood he never settled down to an honest trade, caring only for thrusting spears and wielding staves, and above all he kicked an exceptionally fine game of *qiqiu* football. People in the capital, finding it tripped easily off the tongue, did not call him Gao the Second, but called him Gao Qiu—"Gao the Ball." Later, when he rose in the world, he dropped the "hair" radical from the character for "ball" and added the "standing man" radical, changing his given name to Qiu. This fellow could play wind and string instruments, sing and dance, thrust spears and wield staves, wrestle and frolic, and even had some skill in poetry, prose, lyrics, and rhapsodies. As for benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, trustworthiness, loyalty, and goodness, he knew nothing of them at all. He spent his days sponging off rich young men inside and outside the Eastern Capital. Because he helped the son of Iron-Born Squire Wang squander money day after day in brothels and theaters, the young man's father filed a plaint against him in Kaifeng Prefecture. The Prefect sentenced Gao Qiu to forty strokes of the heavy bamboo on the back and banished him beyond the prefectural borders, forbidding any citizen of the Eastern Capital to give him food or lodging. Having no way out, Gao Qiu had to travel to Linhuai Prefecture in Huaixi and seek shelter with a gambling-den loafer named Eldest Brother Liu, whose given name was Liu Shiquan. All his life Liu loved hosting guests, supporting idlers, and taking in down-and-out drifters from every quarter. Finding refuge in Eldest Brother Liu's house, Gao Qiu stayed there for three years.

Later, when Emperor Zhezong offered sacrifices at the Southern Altar, Heaven was moved to grant seasonable winds and timely rains, and the Emperor proclaimed a broad act of grace and general amnesty throughout the realm. Because his offense was pardoned under the amnesty, Gao Qiu in Linhuai Prefecture grew homesick and wanted to return to the Eastern Capital. Now this Liu Shiquan happened to be a relative of Apothecary Dong, who kept a raw-drug shop beneath Golden Beam Bridge inside the Eastern Capital; so Liu wrote a letter of recommendation, put together some gifts and travel money, and sent Gao Qiu back to the Eastern Capital to seek a livelihood at Apothecary Dong's house.

Taking leave of Eldest Brother Liu and strapping his bundle on his back, Gao Qiu left Linhuai Prefecture and made his way back to the Eastern Capital. He went straight to Apothecary Dong's shop beneath Golden Beam Bridge and delivered the letter. As soon as Apothecary Dong saw Gao Qiu and read Liu Shiquan's letter, he thought to himself: "How can I possibly keep this Gao Qiu in my house? If he were a sincere, honest man, I could let him come and go here and teach my boys some good habits. Instead he is a sponging wastrel with no trustworthiness, and what is more, a man previously convicted and banished from the capital by Kaifeng Prefecture! If I keep him in my house, he will only lead my boys astray. Yet if I refuse to take him in, I cannot save Eldest Brother Liu's face." For the moment he put on a cheerful welcome, kept him in the house to lodge, and entertained him daily with wine and food. After ten days or so, Apothecary Dong thought of a pretext. Bringing out a suit of clothes and writing a letter, he said to Gao Qiu, "My humble household is like the gleam of a firefly—too dim to light a man's path—and I fear keeping you here would spoil your future. Let me pass you on with a recommendation to Junior Academician Su, where in time you may find an official career. How does that strike you?" Overjoyed, Gao Qiu thanked Apothecary Dong. Apothecary Dong sent a servant with the letter to escort Gao Qiu straight to the Academician's mansion. The gatekeeper announced him to Junior Academician Su, who came out, met Gao Qiu, and read the letter. Knowing Gao Qiu had always been an idle loafer, the Academician thought to himself: "How can I keep him here? Better do a favor and recommend him to the mansion of Imperial Son-in-Law Wang Jinqing as a personal attendant. Everyone calls him Junior Commandant Wang, and he likes fellows of this sort." Thereupon he wrote a reply to Apothecary Dong, kept Gao Qiu overnight in his mansion, and the next day wrote a letter of introduction and sent a steward to escort Gao Qiu to Junior Commandant Wang.

This Commandant was brother-in-law to Emperor Zhezong and imperial son-in-law to Emperor Shenzong. He was fond of dashing, stylish company and had just the use for such a man. As soon as he saw the messenger sent by Junior Academician Su with the letter introducing Gao Qiu, he met him and took an immediate liking to him. He wrote a reply on the spot and retained Gao Qiu in his mansion as a personal attendant. From that day on, Gao Qiu enjoyed good fortune in Commandant Wang's household, coming and going just like a member of the family. As the old saying goes: "Days apart make men strangers; days together make men kin." One day, Junior Commandant Wang was celebrating his birthday and ordered a banquet prepared in the mansion, specifically inviting his young brother-in-law, Prince Duan. This Prince Duan was the eleventh son of Emperor Shenzong and younger brother to Emperor Zhezong, then holding court at the Eastern Palace and styled the Ninth Prince. He was a clever, handsome young noble, versed in every trick and pastime of dashing young blades—there was nothing he did not know, nothing he could not do, nothing he did not love—and furthermore accomplished in zither, chess, calligraphy, painting, Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism; as for kicking football, shooting pellets, playing bamboo flutes and tuning strings, singing and dancing, those went without saying. That day in Commandant Wang's mansion a grand banquet was laid out with delicacies of land and sea. Look at it:

> Incense burns in precious tripods; flowers stand in golden vases.
> Musicians of the Celestial Sound Institute vie in playing new airs; performers of the Imperial Music Bureau display their finest arts.
> Inside crystal pitchers is all the jade-nectar of Purple Mansions; within amber cups brims the jewel-liquor of Jasper Pool.
> Tortoiseshell platters heap up fairy peaches and rare fruits; glass bowls serve bear's paws and camel humps.
> Finely sliced raw fish gleams like silver threads; delicately brewed tea unfurls like jade buds.
> Dancing girls in red skirts keep time to ivory clappers and phoenix pipes; singing maidens in emerald sleeves cluster around dragon mouth-organs and phoenix flutes.
> Two rows of pearls and kingfisher plumes stand before the steps; a full ensemble of pipes and strings plays before the seats.

Now Prince Duan arrived at Commandant Wang's mansion for the banquet. The Commandant set out the feast and invited Prince Duan to take the seat of honor in the center, while he sat opposite to keep him company. After several rounds of wine and two courses of dishes, Prince Duan rose to wash his hands. Strolling by chance into the study to rest a moment, he suddenly spotted on the writing desk a pair of paperweight lions carved from mutton-fat jade—exquisitely wrought, delicate and luminous. Prince Duan picked up one of the lions, turning it over and over in his hands without putting it down, and exclaimed, "Splendid!" Seeing how much Prince Duan admired them, Commandant Wang said, "There is also a jade dragon brush-rest made by the very same craftsman, though I do not have it at hand. Tomorrow I will fetch it and send them all to you together." Delighted, Prince Duan said, "Many thanks for your generous kindness. That brush-rest must be even finer." Commandant Wang replied, "Tomorrow I will bring it out and send it to your palace, and you shall see." Prince Duan thanked him again. The two returned to their seats at the banquet and drank until dusk, parting only when thoroughly merry, whereupon Prince Duan took his leave and returned to his palace.

The next day, Junior Commandant Wang brought out the jade dragon brush-rest and the two jade paperweight lions, placed them in a small golden casket, wrapped it in a yellow silk cloth, wrote a covering letter, and sent Gao Qiu to deliver them. Receiving Commandant Wang's order, Gao Qiu took the two jade curios, tucked the letter into his breast, and went straight to Prince Duan's palace. The gate officers announced him to the palace steward. Presently the steward came out and asked, "From whose mansion do you come?" Gao Qiu bowed and replied, "I am from Imperial Son-in-Law Wang's mansion, sent especially to present jade curios to His Highness." The steward said, "His Highness is in the center of the courtyard kicking a football with the young eunuchs. Go on over yourself." Gao Qiu said, "May I trouble you to lead me in?" The steward led him to the courtyard. Looking over, Gao Qiu saw Prince Duan wearing a soft-gauze Tang-style cap and a purple dragon-embroidered robe tied at the waist with a double-tasseled civil-and-military sash, the front skirts of his dragon robe tucked up into his belt, and on his feet a pair of black boots inlaid with gold-thread flying phoenixes. Three to five young eunuchs were playing football with him. Not daring to intrude, Gao Qiu stood behind the attendants awaiting his turn. As luck would have it, Gao Qiu's hour to rise had come: the ball soared into the air, Prince Duan missed his catch, and the ball rolled straight through the crowd to Gao Qiu's feet. Seeing the ball coming, Gao Qiu summoned a sudden burst of nerve and kicked it back to Prince Duan with a "mandarin-duck leg-hook." Overjoyed at the sight, Prince Duan asked, "Who are you?" Stepping forward and kneeling, Gao Qiu said, "Your servant is a personal attendant to Commandant Wang, sent by my master to present two jade curios to Your Highness. Here is a letter of greeting." Hearing this, Prince Duan laughed and said, "My brother-in-law is truly so thoughtful!" Gao Qiu took out the letter and presented it. Prince Duan opened the casket, examined the curios, and handed them all to the hall-attendant to put away.

Without paying further mind to the jade curios, Prince Duan first asked Gao Qiu, "So you know how to kick football! What is your name?" Folding his hands and kneeling in reply, Gao Qiu said, "Your servant is called Gao Qiu. I can kick a few clumsy shots." Prince Duan said, "Good! Step onto the pitch and play a round for fun." Gao Qiu bowed and said, "What sort of person am I, that I should dare play on the same field with Your Grace?" Prince Duan said, "This is the Cloud-Soaring Club, called 'All-Under-Heaven Round'—what harm is there in kicking?" Gao Qiu bowed again and said, "How could I dare!" Three times and five times he begged to be excused, yet Prince Duan insisted he play. Gao Qiu had no choice but to kowtow in apology, loosen his knees, and step onto the field. After just a few kicks, Prince Duan cheered in applause. Gao Qiu put forth every bit of lifelong skill he possessed to entertain Prince Duan; the way he moved his body, the ball seemed glued to him with fish-maw paste. Prince Duan was so delighted he refused to let Gao Qiu return to the Commandant's mansion, keeping him overnight in the palace. The next day he arranged a banquet specifically to invite Commandant Wang to feast in the palace.

Meanwhile, Commandant Wang, seeing that Gao Qiu had not returned that evening, was just wondering why when the gatekeeper reported the next day: "The Ninth Prince has sent a messenger with his princely command inviting Your Lordship to a banquet at the palace." Commandant Wang came out, met the steward, read the command, mounted his horse at once, rode to the Ninth Prince's palace, dismounted, and entered to see Prince Duan. Overjoyed, Prince Duan thanked him for the two jade curios. As they sat feasting and drinking, Prince Duan said, "This Gao Qiu kicks a fine game of football; I would like to ask for him to serve as my personal attendant—what do you say?" Commandant Wang replied, "Since Your Highness has use for him, let him remain right here in the palace to wait upon Your Highness." Pleased, Prince Duan raised his cup in thanks. The two chatted leisurely for a time; at evening the banquet broke up and Commandant Wang returned to his own mansion, of which no more need be said.

From the time Prince Duan obtained Gao Qiu as his companion, he kept him living and eating in the palace. Enjoying Prince Duan's favor, Gao Qiu followed him daily without leaving his side by a single step. Less than two months after he entered the palace, Emperor Zhezong passed away without a crown prince. All the civil and military officials deliberated and enthroned Prince Duan as Son of Heaven, taking the imperial title Huizong—none other than the Daoist Sovereign Emperor, Patriarch of the Jade Clarity Sect. After mounting the throne, all remained peaceful. One day he said to Gao Qiu, "We wish to promote you, yet by rule advancement requires frontier merit. First We will have the Bureau of Military Affairs enter your name on its rolls, so that you may be promoted as an attendant of the imperial cortege." Thereafter, within less than half a year, he promoted Gao Qiu straight to the post of Grand Marshal of the Palace Guard.

Having been appointed Grand Marshal of the Palace Guard, Gao Qiu chose an auspicious day and hour to take up office at the Palace Guard Headquarters. All subordinate clerks, staff officers, metropolitan troops, imperial guards, cavalry, and infantry came to pay homage, each submitting his name-card and roster roll. Marshal Gao checked them off one by one. Among them all, only one man was missing: Wang Jin, Arms Instructor of the Eight Hundred Thousand Imperial Guards. Half a month earlier he had submitted a sick-leave petition on record; his illness had not yet healed, so he had not come to the office to attend to duties. Flying into a rage, Marshal Gao roared, "Nonsense! Even though a petition was submitted, is that wretch not defying authority and fobbing me off? He is plainly feigning illness at home! Arrest him and bring him here at once!" Immediately he dispatched men to Wang Jin's house to seize him.

Now this Wang Jin had neither wife nor child—only an aged mother already past sixty. The squad leader said to Instructor Wang Jin, "Marshal Gao has just taken office today and found you absent at roll call. The Provost Office reported that you are sick at home with a medical petition on file, yet Marshal Gao flew into a fury, refused to believe it, and insists on arresting you, saying you are feigning illness at home. You will have to go show yourself, Instructor. If you do not go, everyone will be implicated, and I too will be punished." Hearing this, Wang Jin had no choice but to drag his sick body along to the Palace Guard Headquarters to pay homage to the Marshal. He bowed four times, straightened up with a respectful greeting, and stood to one side. Gao Qiu said, "So you wretch are the son of Metropolitan Guard Instructor Wang Sheng!" Wang Jin replied, "Your humble servant is indeed." Gao Qiu barked, "You wretch! Your father was a street-corner medicine peddler who twirled fancy staves—what martial arts could you possibly know? The former commander had no eyes, appointing you an instructor! How dare you look down on me and refuse to answer my muster? On whose power and influence do you rely to feign illness at home and take your ease?" Wang Jin pleaded, "How would your servant dare! In truth I have been ill and have not yet recovered." Marshal Gao cursed, "Thieving convict! If you are sick, how were you able to come here?" Wang Jin pleaded again, "When Your Lordship summons, how dare I not come?" Furious, Marshal Gao shouted to his guards to seize Wang Jin: "Beat this wretch hard for me!" All the staff officers were on good terms with Wang Jin, so they joined the Provost Office in pleading: "Today is Your Lordship's auspicious day of taking office; spare this man for this once." Marshal Gao roared, "You thieving convict! Out of regard for these officers I will spare you today's offense, but tomorrow I will settle accounts with you!"

Having thanked him for sparing him, Wang Jin rose, lifted his head to look, and recognized Gao Qiu. Leaving the headquarters, he heaved a sigh and said, "My life will be hard to save this time! I wondered what Marshal Gao this was—and it turns out to be Gao the Second of the Eastern Capital's football club! Years ago, when he was learning to use the staff, my father knocked him down with a single blow so that he lay laid up for three or four months! Bearing that grudge, now that he has risen in the world and become Grand Marshal of the Palace Guard, he is itching to take revenge. Never did I dream I would fall directly under his command! As the old saying goes: 'Fear not the official, fear the one who governs you.' How can I contend with him? What is to be done?" Returning home thoroughly downcast, he told his mother everything. Mother and son clasped each other and wept. His mother said, "My son, of the thirty-six stratagems, running away is the best. I only fear there is nowhere to run." Wang Jin said, "Mother speaks rightly. I have been thinking along the very same lines. Only in Yan'an Prefecture is Old Military Commissioner Zhong guarding the frontier; many of the officers under him have been to the capital and admire my skill with spear and staff. Why not flee there and seek refuge with them? That is a place where men are needed, and we can settle down in peace." Mother and son agreed on the plan. His mother added, "My son, if we slip away secretly, I only fear the two orderlies at the gate assigned by the Palace Guard Headquarters to wait on you. If they find out, we will never get away." Wang Jin said, "No matter—rest easy, Mother, I have my own way of dealing with them."

That evening before dusk, Wang Jin first called Orderly Zhang inside and instructed him, "Eat your supper early; I am sending you somewhere on an errand." Orderly Zhang asked, "Where is the Instructor sending me?" Wang Jin said, "Because of my recent illness, I made a vow to offer incense at the Eastern Peak Temple outside Sour Jujube Gate. Tomorrow morning I mean to go burn the first stick of incense. Go there tonight ahead of me, tell the temple caretaker to open the temple gates a little early tomorrow while I wait to burn the first incense, and prepare three sacrificial meats to offer to Kings Liu and Li. You sleep at the temple tonight and wait for me." Orderly Zhang agreed, ate an early supper, wished him good night, and set off for the temple. That night mother and son packed their luggage, clothes, valuables, and silver into a single carrying-pole load, and also filled two saddlebags to strap onto the horse. Waiting until the fifth watch before dawn, Wang Jin woke Orderly Li and instructed him, "Take this silver to the Eastern Peak Temple, buy the three sacrificial meats with Orderly Zhang, boil them ready, and wait for me there; I will buy paper money and candles and follow shortly." Orderly Li took the silver and headed for the temple. Wang Jin saddled the horse himself, led it out from the rear stable, slung the saddlebags across its back, lashed them tight with cords, led it outside the back gate, and helped his mother mount. Leaving behind all heavy household goods, he locked both front and back doors, shouldered his carrying-pole load, and followed behind the horse. Taking advantage of the pre-dawn fifth watch, they slipped out through West Hua Gate and took the road toward Yan'an Prefecture.

Meanwhile, the two orderlies bought the sacrificial offerings, boiled them ready at the temple, and waited until mid-morning without seeing him arrive. Growing anxious, Orderly Li hurried back to the house to look, only to find the doors locked and no sign of anyone either way. After searching half the day without finding a single person who had seen them, toward evening Orderly Zhang at the Eastern Peak Temple grew suspicious and ran straight back to the house, where he and Orderly Li searched throughout the dusk. When night fell and neither Wang Jin nor his old mother had returned, the two orderlies went the next day to inquire at his relatives' homes, still finding no trace of them. Fearing they would be implicated, they had no choice but to go to the Palace Guard Headquarters and report: "Instructor Wang has abandoned his home and absconded; mother and son have vanished without a trace." Hearing their report, Marshal Gao flew into a rage and roared, "Thieving convict on the run! Let's see where that wretch thinks he can go!" At once he signed an official warrant and dispatched circulars to every prefecture and commandery to arrest the deserter Wang Jin. Because the two orderlies had informed on him, they were spared punishment, of which no more need be said.

Now Instructor Wang and his mother, having left the Eastern Capital, perforce ate when hungry, drank when thirsty, lodged by night, and traveled by day. They had been on the road for more than a month when one day toward dusk, as Wang Jin walked behind his mother's horse with the carrying-pole on his shoulder, he said to her, "Heaven takes pity on us—thank goodness! Mother and son have escaped that snare of Heaven and earth; Yan'an Prefecture is not far ahead now. Even if Marshal Gao sends men to arrest me, they will never catch me!" Mother and son were delighted. Without realizing it, they had missed the regular stage-inn on the road; having walked on into the evening without encountering a single village or hamlet, they had no idea where to find lodging for the night. Just as they were at a loss, they spotted a gleam of lamplight flickering from a grove of trees in the distance. Seeing it, Wang Jin said, "Good! No matter what, let's go there, speak politely, and beg a night's lodging so we can set out early tomorrow." Turning into the woods to look, they found a large manor house surrounded all around by an earthen wall, outside which stood two or three hundred tall willow trees. Look at that manor:

> In front it opens onto the imperial highway; behind it backs against stream-cut ridges.
> All around, green shade hangs thick from willows; on every side, tall pines stand as dark as dye.
> Thatched halls rise high, built after the model of the Five-Elements Mountain Villa; low-eaved pavilions stand directly against hills and over water.
> Around the house corners, cattle and sheep cover the ground; on the threshing floor, geese and ducks gather in flocks; across broad fields and gardens, hired hands and tenants number a thousand.
> The family household is stately and proud; serving maids and houseboys are past counting.
> Truly: the granary has surplus grain so chickens and dogs are well fed; the household holds many books so sons and grandsons are wise.

Arriving before the manor, Instructor Wang knocked at the gate for a long while until a retainer came out. Setting down his carrying-pole load, Wang Jin bowed to him. The retainer asked, "What business brings you to our manor?" Wang Jin replied, "To tell you the truth, my aged mother and I pressed too far along our journey and missed our inn. Arriving here with no village ahead and no tavern behind, we wish to beg a night's lodging at your honorable manor and set out early tomorrow, paying the customary lodging fee. We earnestly beg your kindness and convenience." The retainer said, "If that is the case, wait a moment while I go ask the Old Squire, master of the manor; if he agrees, you are welcome to stay." Wang Jin said again, "Elder brother, please do us this favor." The retainer went inside for a while, then came out and said, "The Old Squire bids you both come in." Wang Jin helped his mother dismount, shouldered his load, led the horse, and followed the retainer inside to the threshing floor, where he set down his load and tied the horse to a willow tree. Mother and son went straight up into the thatched hall to meet the Old Squire.

The Old Squire was nearly sixty, with beard and hair all white, wearing a dust-guarding warm cap, a straight-seamed wide gown, a black silk sash around his waist, and cured-leather boots on his feet. Seeing him, Wang Jin bowed at once. The Old Squire hastily said, "Do not bow, guest, please rise. You are travelers weary from wind and frost—sit down a while." After Wang Jin and his mother had exchanged courtesies, they all sat down. The Old Squire asked, "Where do you come from, and how come you here at dusk?" Wang Jin answered, "My surname is Zhang, originally of the capital. Having lost my capital in trade and having no means of livelihood, I am going to Yan'an Prefecture to seek refuge with relatives. Unexpectedly today we pressed too far on the road and missed our inn, so we came to beg a night's shelter at your honorable manor and will set out early tomorrow, paying the customary lodging fee." The Old Squire said, "No matter at all. Who in this world carries his house on his back when he travels? You mother and son have probably not yet cooked a meal?" He ordered a retainer to prepare supper. Before long a long table was set out in the hall; a retainer carried in a wooden tray with four vegetable dishes and a platter of beef, arranged them on the table, and first warmed wine to go with them. The Old Squire said, "In a country village we have little to offer—please do not take offense." Wang Jin rose and thanked him: "My mother and I have intruded without cause and received such generous kindness—this grace will be hard to repay." The Old Squire said, "Do not speak so; please drink some wine." After urging five or seven cups of wine upon them, rice was brought out and the two ate. Clearing away the bowls and dishes, the Old Squire rose and led Wang Jin and his mother to a guest room to rest. Wang Jin requested, "May I trouble you to have my mother's mount stabled and supplied with hay and fodder, for which I will pay all together?" The Old Squire said, "That is no trouble either. My household also keeps mules and horses; I will have a retainer lead it to the rear stable and feed it with the rest—you need not worry about hay or fodder." Wang Jin thanked him and carried his load into the guest room. A retainer lit a lamp and brought hot water for them to wash their feet, while the Old Squire retired to the inner quarters. Thanking the retainer, Wang Jin and his mother closed the door and settled down to sleep.

The next morning they slept until daybreak without rising. Passing by the guest room, the Old Squire heard Wang Jin and his mother groaning inside. He called out, "Guest, you have overslept—time to get up!" Hearing him, Wang Jin hurried out of the room, bowed to the Old Squire, and said, "I have been up a good while. Last night we caused you much trouble—most unseemly of us." The Old Squire asked, "Who was groaning like that?" Wang Jin replied, "I dare not conceal it from Your Honor: my aged mother, exhausted from saddle and horse, suffered an attack of heart-pain last night." The Old Squire said, "In that case, guest, do not fret. Let your aged mother stay here at my manor for a few days. I have a prescription for heart-pain; I will send a retainer to the county seat to fill it for your mother to take. Tell her to set her heart at ease and convalesce slowly." Wang Jin thanked him.

To cut a long story short, Wang Jin and his mother stayed at the Old Squire's manor taking medicine for five to seven days. Feeling that his mother had recovered from her illness, Wang Jin packed up to depart. That day, having gone to the rear stable to check on his horse, he saw in the open yard a young man stripped to the waist, his body tattooed with blue-green dragons across skin as white as a silver platter, about eighteen or nineteen years old, practicing there with a staff. Watching for a good while, Wang Jin blurted out without thinking, "That staff is well played—only it has flaws and could not beat a true hero." Hearing this, the young man flew into a rage and shouted, "Who are you, daring to mock my skill? I have studied under seven or eight famous masters—I don't believe I am worse than you! Dare you cross staves with me?" Hardly had he spoken when the Old Squire arrived and barked at the youth, "No insolence!" The young man said, "This wretch is unbearable, mocking my staff-work!" The Old Squire asked, "Can it be our guest knows how to use spear and staff?" Wang Jin replied, "I know a little. May I ask, elder sir, who this young man is in your household?" The Old Squire said, "He is my son." Wang Jin said, "Since he is the young master of the house, if he cares to learn, how would it be if I corrected his form?" The Old Squire said, "If you would, nothing could be better!" And he immediately told the youth to bow to him as master. How would the youth consent to bow? Growing angrier still, he cried, "Father, don't listen to this wretch's nonsense! If he can beat this staff of mine, then I will bow to him as master!" Wang Jin said, "Young master, if you do not think it boorish, let us compare a bout with the staff for sport." Right in the middle of the yard, the youth spun his staff like a windmill and shouted at Wang Jin, "Come on! Come on! Whoever hangs back is no hero!" Wang Jin only smiled and refused to make a move. The Old Squire said, "Since our guest is willing to instruct my foolish boy, what harm is there in a bout?" Wang Jin smiled and said, "I fear if I should bruise your son, it would look unseemly." The Old Squire said, "No matter at all. Even if an arm or leg is broken, it will be his own doing." Wang Jin said, "Forgive my presumption." Taking a staff from the weapon rack in hand, he stepped into the open yard and struck an opening stance. Looking him over, the youth came whirling in with his staff straight at Wang Jin. With a sudden *tuo*, Wang Jin trailed his staff and retreated. Brandishing his staff, the youth charged in after him. Spinning around, Wang Jin brought his staff cleaving down toward the empty ground; seeing the blow coming, the youth raised his staff to parry. Instead of striking down, Wang Jin jerked his staff back and thrust straight at the youth's chest; with a single twist and flick, the youth's staff went flying to one side and he tumbled backward onto the ground. Dropping his staff at once, Wang Jin stepped forward to help him up, saying, "Do not take offense! Do not take offense!" Scrambling to his feet, the youth ran to the side, fetched a stool, pressed Wang Jin to sit, and bowed down, saying, "In vain have I studied under so many masters—they were not worth half a copper! Master, I have no choice but to beg your instruction." Wang Jin said, "My mother and I have troubled your honorable house for days with no way to repay your kindness; it is only right I exert myself."

Overjoyed, the Old Squire bade the youth put on his jacket and come together into the rear hall to sit down. He ordered a retainer to slaughter a sheep and lay out wine, food, and fruits, inviting Wang Jin's mother to join the feast as well. The four took their seats. As wine was poured, the Old Squire rose, offered a cup, and said, "For Master to possess such supreme skill, you must surely be an army instructor. My son had eyes yet failed to recognize Mount Tai." Wang Jin smiled and said, "Honest men do not deceive one another, nor clever men conceal the truth. My surname is not Zhang. I am Wang Jin, Arms Instructor of the Eight Hundred Thousand Imperial Guards in the Eastern Capital, handling spear and staff from morning till night. Because a newly appointed Grand Marshal Gao, whom my late father once knocked down, now holds office as Grand Marshal of the Palace Guard and harbors that old grudge, he means to ruin me. Unfortunately I fall directly under his command and cannot contend with him, so my mother and I had no choice but to flee toward Yan'an Prefecture to seek service under Old Military Commissioner Zhong. Little did I expect to come here, meet you father and son, be treated so generously, and have my aged mother's illness cured. Having troubled you these past days is already unseemly. Since your son is willing to learn, I will teach him with all my strength. Only what your son has learned so far is all showy staff-play—good to look at, useless on the battlefield. I will instruct him anew from the ground up." Hearing this, the Old Squire said, "My son, now you see why you lost! Hurry and bow again to your master." The youth bowed once more to Wang Jin. The Old Squire said, "Instructor, my family has lived for generations here on the border of Huayin County, with Mount Shaohua right in front of us. This village is called Shi Family Village; there are three or four hundred households in the village, all surnamed Shi. From childhood my son cared nothing for farming, loving only to thrust spears and wield staves. His mother could not dissuade him and died of vexation. I had no choice but to indulge his bent. No one knows how much money I have spent seeking masters to teach him; I also hired a master tattooist to prick this floral embroidery across his body—nine dragons in all across his shoulders, arms, and chest—so that everyone in the county calls him Nine-Dragon Shi Jin. Since you have come here today, Instructor, it would be wonderful if you would complete his training; I will naturally reward you handsomely." Delighted, Wang Jin said, "Set your heart at ease, Old Squire. Since you speak thus, I will see your son's training through before I leave." From that very day, having eaten and drunk, they kept Instructor Wang and his mother at the manor. Every day Shi Jin begged Instructor Wang to coach him in the Eighteen Martial Arts, and Wang instructed him in every single one from beginning to end. What are those Eighteen Martial Arts?

> Spear, mace, bow, crossbow, and fire-tube;
> Whip, truncheon, sword, chain, and flail;
> Battle-ax, halberd, dagger-ax, and trident;
> Shield, staff, lance, and fork.

So Shi Jin entertained Instructor Wang and his mother daily at the manor while studying martial arts, while Old Squire Shi attended to his duties as village headman in Huayin County, of which no more need be said. Imperceptibly time slipped swiftly by, and more than half a year passed. Truly:

> Sunlight outside the window flits past at the snap of a finger;
> Flower shadows at the banquet shift before the seats.
> Before one cup is drained to pipes and songs,
> Below the steps the morning watch-bell strikes the hour again.

In just over half a year, Shi Jin mastered all eighteen martial arts thoroughly anew, thanks to Wang Jin's wholehearted guidance, which imparted the subtle secrets of every weapon. Seeing that Shi Jin had become thoroughly proficient, Wang Jin thought to himself: "Though life here is good, it is no permanent solution." One day he brought the matter up and took his leave to set out for Yan'an Prefecture. How would Shi Jin let him go? He said, "Master, just stay right here! I will support you and your mother until the end of your natural years—how good would that be?" Wang Jin replied, "Worthy brother, many thanks for your kind heart; staying here is good in every way, yet I fear Marshal Gao's pursuit may reach here and implicate you. Should I cause you to suffer shackles and imprisonment, it would be far from right. Thus I am caught between two difficulties. My heart is set on going to Yan'an Prefecture to seek service under the Old Military Commissioner; that is a frontier post where men are needed, and there I can settle down in peace." Neither Shi Jin nor the Old Squire could prevail upon him to stay, so they could only prepare a farewell banquet and bring out a tray bearing two bolts of satin and a hundred taels of fine silver to thank the master. The next day Wang Jin packed his carrying-pole load and saddled his horse; mother and son took leave of Old Squire Shi and Shi Jin, Wang helped his mother mount, and they set out along the road to Yan'an Prefecture. Shi Jin had a retainer carry the load and personally escorted them for ten *li*, loath in his heart to part. At last Shi Jin bowed in farewell to his master, parted in tears, and returned home with the retainer, while Instructor Wang shouldered his load again, walked beside the horse with his mother, and took the road west into Guanxi.

The story says no more of Wang Jin going to join the frontier army, but tells of Shi Jin returning to the manor, where every day he tempered his strength and stamina. Being in the prime of youth and having neither wife nor child, he rose at midnight to practice martial arts and spent his days behind the manor shooting arrows and galloping horses. Within less than half a year, Shi Jin's father the Old Squire fell ill and could not rise from bed for several days. Shi Jin sent far and wide for physicians to treat him, yet he could not recover; alas, the Old Squire passed away. Shi Jin prepared inner and outer coffins for the burial, invited Buddhist monks to perform meritorious services, observe the seven-day rites, and pray for the Old Squire's soul, and also invited Daoist priests to erect altars and perform offerings to ferry his spirit to Heaven. After holding more than ten full altars of Buddhist and Daoist services, he chose an auspicious day and hour for the funeral procession and burial. All three or four hundred Shi households of the entire village came in mourning white to escort the coffin, laying him to rest in the ancestral graveyard on the hill west of the village. Thereafter there was no one in Shi Jin's household to manage the estate; unwilling to farm, Shi Jin sought only men to spar with in weapons and staff-play.

Three or four months had already passed since Old Squire Shi's death. It was now mid-sixth month, at the height of summer heat. That day, having nothing to pass the time, Shi Jin fetched a folding chair and sat cooling himself under the shade of a willow beside the threshing floor. From the pine grove opposite came a gust of breeze, and Shi Jin called out in approval, "What a cool breeze!" As he sat enjoying the coolness, he saw a man poking his head out and peering furtively around. Shi Jin shouted, "Strange! Who is spying on our manor there?" Leaping to his feet, he stepped around behind the tree, took one look, and recognized the hunter Li Ji, known as Rabbit-Snaring Li Ji. Shi Jin barked, "Li Ji, what are you spying into my manor for? Are you casing the place?" Stepping forward with a respectful greeting, Li Ji said, "Eldest Brother, I was looking for Short Qiu Yilang of your manor to share a bowl of wine. Seeing you cooling yourself here, I dared not come forward and intrude." Shi Jin said, "Let me ask you: in the past you always brought wild game to sell at my manor, and I never shortchanged you. Why haven't you brought any to sell me lately? Are you looking down on me as if I had no money?" Li Ji replied, "How would I dare! I simply haven't had any game lately, so I dared not come." Shi Jin said, "Nonsense! A mountain as huge as Mount Shaohua, stretching so far and wide—don't tell me there isn't a single roebuck or hare on it!" Li Ji said, "So you don't know, Eldest Brother! Just recently a band of outlaws moved onto the mountain and built a stockade up there, gathering five to seven hundred brigands and over a hundred fine horses. The chief chieftain is called Zhu Wu the Divine Strategist; the second is called Chen Da the Stream-Leaping Tiger; the third is called Yang Chun the White-Spotted Snake. Led by these three, they rob homes and plunder estates. Huayin County dares not go up to arrest them and has posted a reward of three thousand strings of cash for anyone who captures them—yet who dares go up and provoke them? That is why we dare not go up the mountain to hunt game. Where would we get any to sell?" Shi Jin said, "I too heard there were outlaws, though I didn't realize those wretches were operating on such a scale. They are bound to make trouble. Li Ji, whenever you get some game in the future, bring some over." Giving a respectful greeting, Li Ji went off.

Returning to the front hall, Shi Jin pondered: "Those wretches are operating on a big scale and are bound to come harass the village." That being so, he ordered his retainers to pick two fat water buffaloes and slaughter them; since the manor had plenty of fine home-brewed wine, he first burned a sheaf of sacrificial paper money and sent retainers to invite all three or four hundred Shi households of the village to assemble in the thatched hall and take seats in order of age. Having retainers pour wine all around, Shi Jin addressed the crowd: "I have heard there are three outlaw chiefs on Mount Shaohua who have gathered five to seven hundred brigands to rob and plunder. Since those wretches are operating on such a scale, sooner or later they are sure to come harass our village. I have invited you all here today to consult together. Should those wretches come, let every household be prepared: when the wooden clapper sounds at my manor, each of you grab spear or staff and come to the rescue; should trouble strike any of your homes, we will do the same. Helping one another in turn, we will defend our village together. If the outlaws come here, I will deal with them myself." Everyone answered, "We village farmers rely solely on you, Eldest Brother, to take command. When the clapper sounds, who would dare not come?" That evening everyone thanked him for the wine and went home to ready their weapons. Thereafter Shi Jin repaired his gates and walls, fortified the manor, strapped his armor, readied his blades and horses, and stood on guard against bandits, of which no more need be said.

Meanwhile, in the Mount Shaohua stronghold, the three chieftains sat in council. The leader, Zhu Wu the Divine Strategist, though lacking martial prowess, was rich in stratagems. Speaking to Chen Da and Yang Chun, Zhu Wu said, "I have just heard that Huayin County has posted a reward of three thousand strings of cash for our capture. I fear that when they come, we will have to fight them. Yet our stronghold is short of money and grain—why not go plunder some to supply the stronghold? If we stockpile grain inside the stockade, we can hold out against government troops when they come." Chen Da the Stream-Leaping Tiger said, "Well spoken! Let's go straight to Huayin County first and 'borrow' grain from them, and see what they do!" Yang Chun the White-Spotted Snake said, "Don't go to Huayin County—go only to Pucheng County, and nothing can go wrong." Chen Da said, "Pucheng County has few households and little money or grain. Better hit Huayin County—the people there are rich and money and grain abound." Yang Chun said, "Brother, you don't realize: if we go attack Huayin County, we must pass right by Shi Family Village. That Nine-Dragon Shi Jin is a fierce tiger—we must not provoke him! How would he ever let us pass?" Chen Da said, "How timid you are, brother! If we can't even pass a single village, how will we ever resist government troops?" Yang Chun said, "Brother, do not underestimate him—that man is truly formidable." Zhu Wu said, "I too have heard he is a great hero, a man of genuine skill. Let's not go, brother." Chen Da shouted out, "Shut your foul mouths, the two of you! Puffing up another's spirit while deflating your own prestige! He is only one man—he doesn't have three heads and six arms! I don't believe it!" And he bellowed to the brigands, "Quickly saddle my horse! We ride right now—first we smash Shi Family Manor, and then we take Huayin County!" Zhu Wu and Yang Chun pleaded with him again and again, yet Chen Da refused to listen. Donning his armor and mounting at once, he mustered a hundred and forty or fifty brigands and marched down the mountain with gongs clanging and drums beating toward Shi Family Village.

Now Shi Jin was inside his manor inspecting blades and horses when a retainer ran in to report the news. Hearing it, Shi Jin immediately sounded the wooden clapper at the manor. From front and back, east and west of the village, hearing the clapper ring out, the three or four hundred Shi households grabbed spears and dragged staves, gathering three or four hundred strong at Shi Family Manor. They saw Shi Jin wearing a flat-topped cap, clad in vermilion armor over a green brocade jacket, shod in emerald-trimmed boots, girded with a leather belt, protected fore and aft by iron heart-guards, carrying a bow and a quiver of arrows, and holding a three-pointed, double-edged, four-holed, eight-ringed halberd. A retainer led out his charcoal-red steed; mounting the horse and leveling his halberd, with thirty or forty sturdy retainers arrayed in front, eighty or ninety tough country lads drawn up behind, and all the Shi villagers following at the rear, Shi Jin led them forward with a unified war-shout to draw up at the northern entrance of the village. Already the oncoming force was in sight. Look at them:

> Red flags flash; crimson banners flutter.
> Brigands brandish forks and spears at random; reckless fellows shoulder blades and axes in a row.
> Headcloths tied askew look like third-month peach blossoms; padded jackets strapped tight resemble ninth-month fallen leaves.
> Every man glares with round, murderous eyes; every heart harbors a yaksha's ferocity.

Leading his men down from Mount Shaohua, Chen Da galloped to the foot of the slope and deployed his brigands. Looking closely, Shi Jin saw Chen Da wearing a dry-red sunken-crown headcloth, clad in gold-inlaid pig-iron armor over a red padded jacket, shod in drop-heel boots, girded with a seven-foot braided-cord belt, riding a tall white horse, and holding a ten-foot-eight steel-tipped lance across his saddle. With brigands shouting war-cries on either flank, the two commanders faced each other on horseback. Looking at Shi Jin from his saddle, Chen Da bowed slightly in courtesy. Shi Jin roared, "You wretches murder, burn, and plunder homes, committing crimes monstrous enough to blot out the sky—every one of you deserves death! Surely you have ears! What gall, coming straight to break ground on the head of Tai Sui himself!"

From his saddle Chen Da replied, "Our mountain stronghold is short of grain, so we wish to go to Huayin County to borrow some. Passing by your honorable manor, we ask only to borrow a road and dare not disturb a single blade of grass. Let us pass through, and on our return we will naturally thank you." Shi Jin said, "Nonsense! My family currently serves as village headman, and I was just about to come arrest you pack of thieves! Today you come marching straight through my village—if instead of arresting you I let you pass, when the county learns of it, I will surely be implicated!" Chen Da said, "Within the four seas all men are brothers—please lend us a road." Shi Jin said, "What idle chatter! Even if I were willing, there is one here who is not. If you can ask him and get his consent, then go." Chen Da asked, "Hero, whom do you bid me ask?" Shi Jin said, "If you can ask this blade in my hand and get its consent, I will let you pass!" Flying into a rage, Chen Da shouted, "Never corner a fleeing man—do not push your luck!" Enraged as well, Shi Jin whirled his halberd and spurred his steed to engage Chen Da, while Chen Da slapped his horse and leveled his lance to meet Shi Jin. The two clashed on horseback. Look at them:

> One thrust, one parry; one rising, one plunging.
> Thrusting and parrying like dragons sporting with a pearl in deep waters; rising and plunging like tigers fighting over prey on a cliffside.
> Wheeling left and circling right, just like Zhang Fei facing Lü Bu; charging forward and spinning back, wholly like Jingde battling Qin Qiong.
> Nine-Dragon in fury aims his three-pointed halberd straight at the crown of his foe's skull; Stream-Leaping Tiger in wrath keeps his eighteen-foot lance fixed on his rival's heart.
> Amid skilled hands, each displays the finer hand; inside the bull's-eye, each strives to seize the dead center.

Shi Jin and Chen Da fought for a long time while their warhorses neighed and reared and the weapons in their hands clashed back and forth in parry and guard. The young woman replied, "Your Honor does not know—allow me to explain. I am from the Eastern Capital, and came here to Weizhou with my parents to seek relatives, only to find they had moved away to Nanjing. My mother fell ill and died in our inn, leaving my father and me stranded here in misery. Now there is a rich man here called Squire Zheng, known as the Lord of the West; seeing me, he used bullying matchmakers and guarantors to force me to become his concubine. Who would have thought that after drawing up a contract for three thousand strings of cash—empty money on a binding deed—he took my body, and before three months were out, his principal wife proved so ferocious that she beat me and drove me out of the house, forbidding me to stay with him, and made the innkeeper press us to repay the original three thousand strings of pledge-money! My father is timid and cannot contend with him, while he has both money and power. We never received a single copper from him at the start—where could we get money to repay him now? Having no other way, my father taught me a few little songs when I was small, so I come to the taverns here to sing for tables. Every day whatever little money we earn, we pay most of it over to him, keeping barely enough for father and daughter to get by. These past two days customers have been scarce, so we missed his payment deadline and fear the shame he will heap on us when he comes to collect. Thinking of this bitterness with nowhere to turn, we could not help weeping. Never did I mean to offend Your Honor—I beg your forgiveness and your generous mercy!" Major Lu asked further, "What is your surname, which inn are you staying at, and where does this Squire Zheng, the Lord of the West, live?" The old man answered, "My surname is Jin, second in my family; my daughter's childhood name is Cuilian. Squire Zheng is Butcher Zheng, who sells meat beneath Champion Bridge here, nicknamed the Lord of the West. My daughter and I lodge just ahead inside the East Gate at the Lu Family Inn." Hearing this, Lu Da spat, "Bah! I thought that Squire Zheng was somebody grand, and he's just Zheng the pork-butcher! That filthy, shameless wretch sets up a meat stall under the patronage of our Junior Military Commissioner, yet dares bully people like this!" Turning to Li Zhong and Shi Jin, he said, "The

two of you stay right here while I go beat that wretch to death and come back!" Shi Jin and Li Zhong held him back, urging, "Calm your anger, elder brother—we can deal with it tomorrow." Three times and five times they pleaded before finally holding him back.

Lu Da said again, "Come here, old man—I will give you some travel money so you can set out for the Eastern Capital tomorrow. How about that?" Father and daughter cried, "If we could return to our home village, you would be like reborn parents to us! Only how would the innkeeper let us go? Squire Zheng holds him responsible for the money." Major Lu said, "That is no trouble—I have my own way to handle it." Reaching into his robe, he pulled out five taels of silver, placed them on the table, and looked at Shi Jin: "I didn't bring much silver with me today. If you have any, lend me some and I will repay you tomorrow." Shi Jin said, "What does this trifle matter, that you should repay me?" Taking a ten-tael ingot of silver from his bundle, he placed it on the table. Lu Da looked at Li Zhong: "You lend me some too!" Reaching into his pocket, Li Zhong pulled out two taels of silver. Glancing at it and seeing how little it was, Lu Da muttered, "Another stingy fellow!" Giving only the fifteen taels to Old Jin, Lu Da instructed, "Take this for your travel expenses, go pack your luggage, and early tomorrow morning I will come see you two off—let's see which innkeeper dares detain you!" Old Jin and his daughter bowed in gratitude and departed.

Tossing the two taels of silver back to Li Zhong, Lu Da drank two more measures of wine with his companions before they went downstairs, where he called out, "Landlord, I will bring your wine money tomorrow." The landlord answered repeatedly, "Go right ahead, Major, drink as much as you like—my only fear is that Your Honor won't run a tab!" Leaving the Pan family tavern, the three parted on the street: Shi Jin and Li Zhong returned to their respective inns, while Major Lu went back to his lodgings in front of the Military Commissioner's Headquarters, went straight to his room without eating supper, and went to bed fuming, the landlord not daring to ask him a word.

Meanwhile, having received the fifteen taels of silver, Old Jin returned to the inn, settled his daughter, went first to the far outskirts of town to hire a cart for the morning, came back and packed their luggage, paid off their room rent, and settled their firewood and rice bills, waiting only for daybreak. Nothing happened that night. Rising at the fifth watch the next morning, father and daughter lit the stove, cooked and ate their breakfast, and gathered their things. Just as the sky grew faintly light, Major Lu strode briskly into the inn, shouting loudly, "Waiter, where is Old Jin lodging?" The waiter called, "Old Master Jin, the Major is here looking for you!" Opening his door, Old Jin said, "Please step inside and sit down, Your Honor." Lu Da said, "Why sit? If you're leaving, leave now—what are you waiting for?" Leading his daughter and shouldering his load, Old Jin thanked the Major and started for the door, when the waiter stepped in front of him: "Where are you going, Old Master Jin?" Lu Da asked, "Does he owe you room money?" The waiter said, "He settled my room money last night, but he still owes Squire Zheng's pledge-money, and I am held responsible for keeping watch over him!" Major Lu said, "I will pay Zheng's money myself—let this old man go back to his home village!" When the waiter still refused to let them pass, Lu Da flew into a rage, spread his five fingers, and slapped the waiter across the face so hard blood gushed from his mouth; following with a single punch, he knocked out his two front teeth. Scrambling up, the waiter bolted away like a wisp of smoke, while the innkeeper dared not step out to stop them. Hastily leaving the inn, Old Jin and his daughter hurried out of the city to find the cart hired the day before.

Now Lu Da reflected that the waiter might run ahead to intercept them, so he pulled up a stool in the inn and sat there for two full double-hours. Estimating that Old Jin must be far away by now, he finally rose and headed straight for Champion Bridge. There Butcher Zheng kept a two-bay shopfront with two chopping blocks, hung with three or five sides of pork; Zheng himself sat behind the front counter watching ten or more journeymen sell meat. Walking up to the door, Lu Da called out, "Butcher Zheng!" Looking up and seeing Major Lu, Butcher Zheng hurried out from behind the counter, bowed respectfully, and said, "Forgive me, Major!" Turning to an assistant, he called, "Bring a stool for the Major to sit on!" Sitting down, Lu Da said, "By order of His Excellency the Military Commissioner, I want ten catties of lean meat minced into fine stuffing—not a speck of fat to be seen in it!" Butcher Zheng said, "Headman, quickly pick the best lean meat and mince ten catties!" Major Lu said, "Don't let those filthy louts touch it—you mince it for me yourself!" Butcher Zheng said, "You are right, I will mince it myself." Going to the block, he selected ten catties of lean pork and minced it finely into stuffing. Meanwhile, the inn waiter, his head wrapped in a kerchief, had come to Butcher Zheng's shop to report Old Jin's departure, but seeing Major Lu sitting right beside the meat block, he dared not approach and stood watching from afar beneath the eaves. Butcher Zheng spent a full half double-hour mincing the meat, wrapped it in lotus leaves, and asked, "Major, shall I send someone to deliver it?" Lu Da said, "Deliver what? Hold on—now I want another ten catties of pure fat, with not a shred of lean in it, also minced into fine stuffing!" Butcher Zheng said, "The lean meat just now was no doubt for making wontons at the headquarters—what use is minced fat?" Glaring at him, Lu Da said, "His Excellency gave me his order—who dares question him?" Butcher Zheng said, "If it is needed, I will mince it right away." Picking out ten catties of solid fatback, he minced it finely too and wrapped it in lotus leaves. By the time he had spent the whole morning at it, it was past the breakfast hour; the inn waiter dared not come near, and even customers coming to buy meat dared not approach. Butcher Zheng said, "Let me have someone carry these for the Major and deliver them to the headquarters." Lu Da said, "Now I want another ten catties of pure gristle, chopped just as fine into stuffing, with not a bit of meat showing!" Laughing, Butcher Zheng said, "Aren't you deliberately making sport of me?" Hearing this, Lu Da leaped to his feet, grabbed the two packages of minced meat in his hands, glared at Butcher Zheng, and roared, "I am deliberately making sport of you!" And he flung both packages straight into Butcher Zheng's face like a sudden shower of meat! Enraged, Butcher Zheng felt two jets of fury shoot from the soles of his feet to the crown of his head, and the unquenchable fire of wrath blazed uncontrollably in his heart; snatching a sharp boning knife from the meat block, he vaulted out from behind the counter. Major Lu had already stepped back into the middle of the street. None of the neighbors or dozen shop-hands dared step forward to intervene; passersby on both sides halted in their tracks, and the inn waiter stood frozen in terror.

With the knife in his right hand, Butcher Zheng reached out with his left to grab Lu Da. Seizing his chance, Major Lu clamped down on his left wrist, stepped in close, and kicked him squarely in the lower belly, sending him sprawling flat in the middle of the street. Taking another stride forward, Lu Da planted his foot on Zheng's chest, raised a fist the size of a vinegar jar, and glared down at him: "When I served under the Old Military Commissioner, I rose to be Investigating Commissioner of the Five Circuits of Guanxi, so calling me Lord of the West was no empty title! You are nothing but a knife-wielding pork-butcher, a dog of a fellow—and you dare call yourself Lord of the West? How dare you forcibly defraud Jin Cuilian!" *Thud!* Down came a single punch squarely on the bridge of his nose, flattening it to one side while fresh blood spurted out—just as if he had opened a condiment shop, with salty, sour, and spicy flavors all pouring out together! Unable to rise, his knife dropped aside, Butcher Zheng could only groan, "Well struck!" Lu Da cursed, "Filthy mother-robber, still daring to talk back!" Raising his fist, he landed a single blow right across the corner of his eye and brow, splitting the eyelid wide and popping the eyeball half out—just as if he had opened a silk-and-dyes shop, with red, black, and crimson all spilling out together! Onlookers on both sides, terrified of Major Lu, dared not step forward to part them. Unable to bear any more, Butcher Zheng begged for mercy. Lu Da roared, "Bah! You miserable wretch—if you had toughed it out to the end with me, I might have spared you; since you beg me for mercy, I will not spare you!" With one more punch squarely on the temple, it was as if a full Buddhist service for land and water had broken out, with chimes, cymbals, and hand-bells all clanging at once! Looking down, Lu Da saw Butcher Zheng stretched stiff on the ground, breath coming out of his mouth with none going in, unable to stir. Pretending to shout, "You wretch are playing dead—I'll beat you some more!" Lu Da saw his skin steadily changing color and thought to himself: "I only meant to give this wretch a thorough thrashing—I didn't expect three punches would actually kill him! I'll be hauled before the court with no one to bring me food in jail—better clear out right now!" Turning on his heel, he strode away, pointing back over his shoulder at Butcher Zheng's corpse and shouting, "Play dead if you like—I'll settle accounts with you later!" Cursing as he walked, he disappeared with great strides while none of the neighbors or Butcher Zheng's shop-hands dared lift a finger to stop him.

Returning to his lodgings, Major Lu hastily rolled up a few clothes, travel money, and fine silver, abandoned all his old garments and heavy gear, picked up a brow-high cudgel, bolted out the South Gate, and vanished like a wisp of smoke. Meanwhile, Butcher Zheng's household tried in vain for half the day to revive him, only to find him dead; family and neighbors went straight to the prefectural yamen to file a plaint. Just then the Prefect was holding court; receiving the plaint and reading it, he said, "Lu Da is a Major in the Military Commissioner's Headquarters—I dare not arrest the killer on my own authority." Mounting his sedan chair at once, the Prefect went to the Military Commissioner's Headquarters, alighted, and was announced by the gate guards. Hearing of his arrival, the Military Commissioner invited him into the hall; after exchanging courtesies, the Commissioner asked, "What brings you here?" The Prefect reported, "I must inform Your Excellency: Major Lu Da of your headquarters has without cause beaten Butcher Zheng to death with his fists in the marketplace. Not having reported to Your Excellency, I dared not arrest the culprit on my own authority." Startled by the news, the Military Commissioner thought to himself: "Though this Lu Da excels in martial arts, his temper is coarse and reckless. Now that he has committed manslaughter, how can I shield him? I must let him be examined." He replied to the Prefect, "This Lu Da was originally an officer under my father, the Old Military Commissioner; because I was short of capable assistants here, he was transferred to serve as Major. Since he has committed a capital crime, you may arrest him and examine him according to law. Once his confession is clear and his sentence determined, let my father be informed before final execution, lest my father ask for this man from the frontier later and things look awkward." The Prefect replied, "Once I have examined the facts, I will duly report to His Excellency the Old Military Commissioner before passing final sentence." Taking leave of the Commissioner, the Prefect mounted his sedan chair, returned to the prefectural yamen, ascended the bench, summoned the duty constable-inspector, and signed a warrant for the arrest of the culprit Lu Da.

Taking the warrant and twenty-odd constables, Inspector Wang went straight to Major Lu's lodgings, only to hear the landlord say, "He just left carrying a bundle and a short cudgel; I thought he was on official duty and dared not question him." Inspector Wang ordered his door forced open, finding nothing inside save old clothes and bedding. Taking the landlord with him, Inspector Wang searched east, west, north, and south across the entire city without finding a trace. Arresting two neighboring households along with the landlord, Inspector Wang returned to the prefectural court and reported: "Major Lu has absconded in fear of punishment and his whereabouts are unknown; we have only arrested his landlord and neighbors here." The Prefect ordered them held in custody for the time being, summoned Butcher Zheng's neighbors, assigned the coroner and ward officers alongside the local ward headman to examine the corpse thoroughly, and instructed Zheng's family to place the body in a coffin and lodge it in a monastery. Having compiled the case dossier, he set a beaten deadline for his constables to apprehend the killer, released the plaintiffs on bail, sentenced the immediate neighbors to the bamboo for failing to come to the rescue, found the landlord and lodging-house neighbors guilty only of minor negligence, and issued a general hue-and-cry warrant throughout the realm for the fugitive Lu Da, offering a reward of a thousand strings of cash, listing his age, appearance, and native place, and posting portraits of him everywhere. The remaining witnesses were released to await summons, while Butcher Zheng's relatives went home to observe mourning, of which no more need be said.

Meanwhile, having left Weizhou, Lu Da fled east and west, just like:

> A lone wild goose separated from its flock, skimming the sky alone by moonlight;
> A live fish escaped from the net, flipping its body to leap through surging waves.
> Caring nothing for distance, heedless of high or low;
> Knocking down passersby in his haste, running fleet as a warhorse charging into battle.

Hurrying like a homeless dog and darting like a fish slipped through the net, Major Lu passed through several prefectures and commanderies—truly, fleeing for life chooses no road, and everywhere becomes home. As the ancients said: the hungry choose not their food, the cold choose not their clothes, the panicked choose not their road, and the poor choose not their wife. Pressing forward in anxious haste with no clear destination in mind, Lu Da traveled blindly for more than half a month until he arrived at Yanmen County in Daizhou. Entering the city, he saw bustling markets, teeming crowds, carriages and horses racing side by side, and all hundred and twenty trades of commerce and merchandise neatly arrayed—though only a county seat, it surpassed a prefectural capital. As Major Lu walked along, he noticed a crowd gathered at a crossroads reading a public notice. Look at them:

> Leaning shoulder to shoulder, craning neck beside neck.
> Swarming together, wise cannot be told from foolish; jostling in crowds, noble cannot be told from base.
> Clumsy fat Zhang San, illiterate, only shakes his head; short, squat Li Si, watching others, stands on tiptoe too.
> White-haired old men rest their whiskers upon their walking sticks; dark-haired young scholars copy out the clauses with brush and ink.
> Every line upholds the laws of Xiao He; every sentence follows the statutes of the realm.

Seeing the crowd packed around the crossroads reading the notice, Lu Da squeezed into the throng to listen; being illiterate himself, he heard the crowd reading aloud: "Yanmen County of Daizhou, in compliance with instructions from the Taiyuan Prefectural Command forwarding a dispatch from Weizhou, orders the arrest of Lu Da, killer of Butcher Zheng and former Major in the Military Commissioner's Headquarters. Whoever harbors him at home with food or lodging shares his crime; whoever captures him or informs the authorities shall be paid a reward of one thousand strings of cash." Just as Major Lu was listening to that point, a man behind him shouted aloud, "Eldest Brother Zhang, what are you doing here?" Throwing both arms around his waist, he dragged him straight toward the county yamen.

Had it not been for this man spotting him and hauling him away bodily, how would it come to pass that Major Lu would shave off his hair, shear away his beard, change his murderous name, and vex all the Buddhas and Arhats to distraction—until his Zen staff beat open perilous paths and his precept-blade slew every unjust man?

## Chapter 4: Squire Zhao Rebuilds Wenshu Monastery; Lu Zhishen Raises Havoc on Mount Wutai

{{4}} A poem says:

> Fleeing trouble and escaping disaster, he enters Daizhou;
> Meeting one he saved, gratitude is warmly repaid.
> Because the net of the law is spread fold upon fold,
> He enters the Gate of Emptiness to cultivate himself well.
> Sitting in meditation and pondering Chan, seeking liberation;
> Passing springs and autumns on coarse tea and plain rice.
> In years to come, when fruit is attained and worldly ties are fulfilled,
> He will journey at ease into Amitabha's Pure Land.

The story goes that when Major Lu twisted around to look, the man dragging him was none other than Old Jin, whom he had rescued in the Weizhou tavern! Pulling Lu Da straight into a quiet alley, the old man said, "Benefactor, what nerve you have! Right there in plain sight hangs the reward notice offering a thousand strings of cash for your capture—why on earth did you go look at the notice? If I hadn't run into you, the constables would surely have grabbed you! Your age, looks, and native place are all written right on it." Lu Da said, "To tell you the truth, on your account I went back beneath Champion Bridge that very day, ran straight into that wretch Butcher Zheng, and killed him with three punches. That is why I am on the run. I have been drifting around for forty or fifty days without expecting to end up here. Why didn't you go back to the Eastern Capital instead of coming here?" Old Jin said, "Hear me, Benefactor: after you saved us, I hired a cart and meant to return to the Eastern Capital, yet I feared that wretch might pursue us and you would no longer be there to rescue us. So instead of going to the Eastern Capital, we followed the road northward. Along the way we ran into an old neighbor from the capital who was coming here to trade, and he brought my daughter and me along. Thanks to him, he acted as matchmaker for my daughter and arranged a match with a wealthy gentleman here, Squire Zhao, who maintains her in a separate residence. Our abundant food and clothing all come from you, Benefactor! My daughter constantly speaks of your great kindness to her gentleman, and the Squire himself loves spear-play and staff-work; he often says, 'How I wish I could meet our benefactor face to face!' We wondered how we could ever see you. Please come to our house first, and in a few days we can decide what to do."

Major Lu walked less than half a *li* with Old Jin before arriving at the doorway. Lifting the door-curtain, the old man called out, "My daughter, our great benefactor is here!" Richly dressed and adorned, the young woman came out from within, invited Lu Da to sit in the center, and bowed six times as straight and earnest as planted candles, saying, "Had you not stooped to rescue us, Benefactor, how could we have reached this day?" Looking at the young woman, Lu Da saw a wholly different grace and poise than before. Look at her:

> A golden hairpin slants through dark, shadowing clouds of hair; kingfisher-green sleeves, deftly tailored, lightly veil auspicious snow.
> A cherry mouth faintly tinted with delicate rouge; spring-bamboo-shoot fingers half-unfolding tender jade.
> A slender waist sways gracefully, a green silk skirt revealing tiny golden lotuses; a fair frame moves lightly, a red embroidered jacket setting off her jade-like form.
> Her cheeks bloom with third-month flowers; her brows sweep like early-spring willows; fragrant skin shimmers like Jade Terrace moonlight, emerald locks billow like clouds on Mount Wu.

Having finished bowing, the young woman invited Major Lu, "Please come upstairs and be seated, Benefactor." Lu Da said, "Do not put yourselves out—I must be on my way shortly." Old Jin insisted, "Now that you have come here, Benefactor, how could we let you just walk away?" Taking his staff and bundle, the old man ushered him upstairs to sit down and told his daughter, "Stay and keep our benefactor company while I go prepare something." Lu Da said, "No need to go to trouble—whatever is handy will do." The old man said, "Not even giving my life could repay your kindness, Major—how could coarse food and plain fare be worth mentioning?" While the young woman kept Lu Da seated upstairs, Old Jin went down, called a newly hired serving boy, told the maidservant to kindle the fire, and went out onto the street with the boy to buy fresh fish, tender chicken, stuffed goose, rich pickled fish, and seasonal fresh fruits. Returning, he opened wine, prepared vegetable dishes, arranged everything neatly, and carried it upstairs. On the spring-wood table were set three cups and three pairs of chopsticks, laid out with vegetables, fruits, and meats; the maid poured wine from a silver pitcher, and father and daughter took turns offering him cups. Old Jin dropped to the floor in another kowtow. Major Lu said, "Old sir, why bow like this? You overwhelm me!" Old Jin replied, "Hear me, Benefactor: the very first day I arrived here, I wrote out a red paper tablet with your name and burned a stick of incense morning and evening while my daughter and I bowed before it. Today, when you are here in person, how could I not bow?" Lu Da said, "Rare indeed is such a faithful heart as yours." The three drank leisurely together.

Toward evening, an uproar suddenly broke out downstairs. Opening the window to look down, Major Lu saw twenty or thirty men below, each armed with a white wooden cudgel, all shouting, "Drag him down!" In the middle of the crowd sat a gentleman on horseback roaring, "Don't let that thief escape!" Seeing trouble brewing, Lu Da snatched up a stool and started down the stairs to smash them. Clapping his hands frantically, Old Jin shouted, "Nobody lift a hand!" Rushing down the stairs, the old man ran straight up to the gentleman on horseback and spoke a few words; at once the gentleman burst out laughing and waved away the twenty or thirty retainers, who dispersed.

Dismounting and entering the house, the gentleman waited while the old man invited Major Lu downstairs. Dropping immediately to his knees, the gentleman bowed and said, "Hearing a name cannot match seeing the face, and seeing the face surpasses hearing the name! Accept my bow, righteous Major!" Lu Da asked Old Jin, "Who is this gentleman? We have never met—why does he bow to me?" The old man answered, "This is my daughter's gentleman, Squire Zhao. Just now someone misinformed him that I was entertaining some young gallant upstairs with wine, so he brought his retainers to start a fight. Once I explained, he sent them away." Lu Da said, "So that's how it is—the Squire cannot be blamed." Squire Zhao invited Major Lu back upstairs to sit down; Old Jin set out fresh cups and platters and prepared more wine and food. Squire Zhao urged Lu Da to take the seat of honor. Lu Da said, "How could I dare?" The Squire said, "Allow me to show a younger brother's respect. I have long heard of your heroism, Major; that Heaven grants us this meeting today is truly my supreme good fortune." Lu Da said, "I am a rough, unpolished fellow who has committed a capital crime; if you do not despise my poverty and low estate and will be my friend, wherever you have need of me, I will go with you." Overjoyed, Squire Zhao inquired about the killing of Butcher Zheng; they chatted leisurely and compared notes on spear-play, drinking halfway through the night before retiring to rest.

At daybreak the next morning, Squire Zhao said, "This place may not be safe—may I invite you, Major, to stay at my humble manor for a time?" Lu Da asked, "Where is your honorable manor?" The Squire replied, "A little over ten *li* from here, at a place called Seven Treasures Village." Lu Da said, "Splendid!" Sending a servant ahead to the manor to bring two horses, before noon the horses arrived. Inviting Major Lu to mount and having a retainer shoulder his baggage, Squire Zhao accompanied Lu Da as he took leave of Old Jin and his daughter, and the two rode side by side toward Seven Treasures Village, chatting along the way. Before long they arrived at the manor gate, dismounted, and Squire Zhao took Lu Da by the hand into the thatched hall, where they sat as host and guest. Ordering a sheep slaughtered and wine set out to entertain him, he had a guest room readied that night and laid out another feast the next day. Lu Da said, "How can I ever repay your misplaced kindness, Squire?" Squire Zhao answered, "Within the four seas all men are brothers—why speak of repayment?"

To cut a long story short, Lu Da stayed at Squire Zhao's manor for five to seven days. One day, as the two were sitting and chatting in the study, Old Jin came hurrying breathlessly to the manor and walked straight into the study to see Squire Zhao and Major Lu. Seeing no outsiders present, he said to Lu Da, "Benefactor, it is not that I am overly suspicious, but the other day when I invited you upstairs for wine, the Squire received a false report and brought his retainers to raise a commotion in the street before dispersing them. People grew suspicious and began gossiping. Yesterday three or four constables came around questioning the neighbors closely; I fear they may come out to the village to search for you, Benefactor! Should anything go amiss, what would we do?" Lu Da said, "In that case, I will simply leave on my own." Squire Zhao said, "If I keep you here, Major, I fear some mishap might befall you and give you cause to reproach me; yet if I do not shelter you, neither of us saves face. I do have a plan that will keep you completely safe and give you a secure refuge—I only fear you may refuse." Lu Da said, "I am a man with a price on my head—so long as I find a place to lay my head, why would I refuse?" Squire Zhao said, "If so, nothing could be better! Thirty-odd *li* from here stands a mountain called Mount Wutai, on which sits Wenshu Monastery, an ancient sanctuary of the Bodhisattva Manjusri. Five to seven hundred monks live in the monastery, and the abbot, Elder Zhizhen, is a sworn brother of mine. My ancestors donated money to the temple and are registered patrons and benefactors there; I myself once vowed to sponsor the ordination of one monk at the monastery and have already purchased a five-flower ordination certificate, lacking only a trusted friend to fulfill that vow. If you are willing, Major, I will furnish every single expense. Would you truly consent to shave your head and become a monk?" Lu Da reflected: "If I leave right now, where can I turn? Better take this road!" So he said, "Since you take charge of it, Squire, I gladly consent to become a monk and will rely wholly on your care." Settling the matter on the spot, they packed clothes, travel money, bolts of silk, and gifts into carrying-loads that very night. Rising early the next morning, they had retainers shoulder the loads and set out toward Mount Wutai, reaching the foot of the mountain shortly after the *chen* watch. Gazing up at Mount Wutai, Major Lu saw it was indeed a mighty mountain! Look at it:

> Clouds veil its peaks; the sun wheels around its waist.
> Towering and jagged, it seems to touch the gates of Heaven; sheer and uneven, it pierces the Milky Way.
> Before the crags, flowers and trees dance in the spring breeze, breathing hidden fragrance; at cavern mouths, vines and creepers draped in overnight rain hang like tender threads.
> Flying clouds and waterfalls chill the moonlight mirrored in the Silver River; sheer cliffs and ancient pines sway iron corner-bells like moving dragon tails.
> Fit to have been dyed in indigo by human hand, yet fashioned by Heaven's craft into piled-up emerald.
> Its broad base presses down three thousand fathoms; its majesty swallows whole four hundred prefectures.

Squire Zhao and Major Lu were carried up the mountain in two sedan chairs while a retainer ran ahead to announce them. Arriving before the monastery, they were met at once by the prior and the superintendent monk. Alighting from their chairs, the two sat down in the pavilion outside the mountain gate. Informed of their arrival, Elder Zhizhen came out beyond the gate leading the senior monk and attendants to welcome them. Squire Zhao and Lu Da stepped forward and bowed; Elder Zhizhen returned a Buddhist greeting and said, "No easy journey for our patron from afar." Squire Zhao answered, "A small matter brings me to trouble your noble monastery." Elder Zhizhen said, "First let the Squire come to the abbot's quarters for tea." With Squire Zhao walking ahead and Lu Da following behind, they entered Wenshu Monastery—truly a grand monastery! Look at it:

> The mountain gate encroaches on steep ridges; the Buddha halls touch the blue clouds.
> The bell tower links with moonlit grottoes; the sutra pavilion stands opposite towering peaks.
> The fragrant kitchen draws water from a clear spring; the monks' quarters gather mist and rosy clouds on all four sides.
> The old abbot's chamber hangs halfway up the slope; Chan meditators' halls sit wrapped in cloud and fog.
> White-faced gibbons offer fruit at every hour, striking wooden fish-drums with strange stones; yellow-dappled deer carry flowers day by day, making offerings before golden Buddhas in jeweled halls.
> A seven-story jeweled pagoda pierces the crimson sky; saintly monks of a thousand ages have graced this great monastery.

Elder Zhizhen ushered Squire Zhao and Lu Da into the abbot's quarters and invited the Squire to sit in the guest seat, whereupon Lu Da promptly sat down in a Chan chair on the lower side. Calling Lu Da over, the Squire whispered in his ear, "You have come here to leave the household life—how can you sit right down facing the Abbot?" Lu Da muttered, "I didn't know," rose, and stood behind the Squire's shoulder. Before them stood the senior monk, precentor, attendants, superintendent, prior, guest-master, and secretary lined up in eastern and western ranks. Having parked the sedan chairs, the retainers carried the gift hampers into the abbot's quarters and set them out in front. The Abbot said, "Why bring gifts again? This monastery has troubled our patron far too often." Squire Zhao replied, "A trifling gift—hardly worth mentioning." Lay brothers and novices cleared the hampers away. Rising, Squire Zhao said, "I have a matter to lay before the Reverend Abbot: I have long held a vow to sponsor the ordination of one monk in your noble monastery, and both ordination certificate and register are already prepared, though until now I have found no one to tonsure. Here is my cousin, surnamed Lu and named Da, a military man by background; seeing the hardships of this dusty world, he wishes of his own free will to renounce secular life and become a monk. I earnestly beg the Elder to accept him—mercy, mercy! Out of regard for my humble face, shave his head and ordain him; I will personally furnish everything he needs. I beg the Elder to grant this wish—how fortunate we shall be!" Hearing this, the Abbot replied, "This karma brings luster to my humble mountain gate—easily done, easily done! First let us take tea." At once a novice brought out tea on a tray. How fine was that cup of tea? A poem bears witness:

> Jade buds and golden shoots, truly a peerless grade;
> Prepared by monks with utmost skill and care.
> Inside the hare's-fur bowl, fragrant clouds rise white;
> Amid crab's-eye simmering water, fine ripples spread.
> It drives the demon of sleep from pillow and mat,
> And infuses pure vital air into skin and frame.
> Such fairy tea belongs by nature to Peach Blossom Spring,
> Forbidden to transplant beside the imperial capital.

When Elder Zhizhen, Squire Zhao, and the others had finished their tea and the saucers were cleared, the Abbot summoned the senior monk and precentor to arrange the tonsure, instructing the superintendent and prior to prepare a vegetarian feast. Meanwhile, the senior monk conferred privately with the other monks: "This fellow looks nothing like a man renouncing the world—his pair of eyes look just like a bandit's!" The monks said, "Guest-master, invite the guests to sit in the parlor while we talk this over with the Abbot." Coming out, the guest-master invited Squire Zhao and Lu Da into the guest hall, while the senior monk and monks addressed the Abbot: "That man who wishes to become a monk just now is coarse and ugly in appearance and fierce and stubborn in looks—he must not be ordained, lest in time he bring trouble upon our mountain gate!" The Abbot said, "He is the cousin of our patron Squire Zhao—how can we refuse to save his face? Put aside your doubts for a moment and let me take a look." Lighting a stick of incense, the Abbot mounted his Chan chair, sat cross-legged, chanted a mantra, and entered meditative trance. After one stick of incense had burned down, he returned from trance and said to the monks, "Ordain him without hesitation! This man answers to a star in Heaven and has an upright, unyielding heart. Though fierce and unruly at present and tangled in his karmic destiny, in the end he will attain purity and achieve an extraordinary fruition that none of you can match. Remember my words and raise no obstruction!" The senior monk said, "The Abbot is simply shielding his favorite, so we have no choice but to obey—it would be wrong not to remonstrate, yet since he will not listen, so be it."

The Abbot ordered vegetarian food prepared and invited Squire Zhao and his party to dine in the abbot's quarters. After the meal, the superintendent drew up an itemized list; Squire Zhao took out silver and sent men to buy materials, while inside the monastery tailors made monk's shoes, monk's robes, a monk's cap, a surplice, and a bowing mat, completing everything within a day or two. Choosing an auspicious day and hour, the Abbot ordered the great bell tolled and the dharma drum beaten, assembling the whole community in the Dharma Hall. Five or six hundred monks, neatly arrayed and all wearing surplices, bowed with joined palms before the dharma throne and stood in two ranks. Taking out silver ingots and offering incense, Squire Zhao bowed before the dharma throne; once the cantor had read out the petition, novices led Lu Da to the foot of the throne. The precentor had Lu Da remove his headcloth, parted his hair into nine tufts, and bound them up. First the barber shaved all around his scalp; as he was about to shave off his beard and mustache, Lu Da said, "Leave those few hairs for me, won't you?" The monks could scarcely stifle their laughter. From the dharma throne Elder Zhizhen said, "Let the assembly hear a gatha," and chanted:

> "Not a blade of grass remains; the six sense-roots are pure.
> Shaving them clean for you spares future strife."

Having chanted the gatha, the Abbot shouted, "Out with it—shave it all off!" With one sweep of his razor, the barber shaved everything clean. The senior monk presented the blank ordination certificate before the dharma throne and asked the Abbot to bestow a dharma name. Holding the blank certificate, the Abbot spoke a gatha:

> "A single spark of spirit-light is worth a thousand pieces of gold;
> Vast is the Buddha-dharma: I bestow the name Zhishen."

Having bestowed the name, the Abbot passed the certificate down; the scribe filled it out and handed it to Lu Zhishen for safekeeping. The Abbot also bestowed a dharma robe and surplice and bade Zhishen put them on. Guided by the superintendent before the dharma throne, the Abbot laid his hand upon Zhishen's crown to confer the precepts, saying: "First, take refuge in the Three Jewels; second, revere the Buddha-dharma; third, honor teachers and friends. These are the Three Refuges. As for the Five Precepts: first, do not take life; second, do not steal; third, do not commit sexual misconduct; fourth, do not indulge in wine; fifth, do not speak falsehood." Unfamiliar with the Chan custom of answering "Yes" or "No," Zhishen simply said, "I'll remember that!"—at which all the monks laughed. Once the ordination was complete, Squire Zhao invited all the monks into the Cloud Hall, burned incense, offered a vegetarian feast, and presented congratulatory gifts to every office-bearing monk great and small. The prior led Lu Zhishen around to bow to all his senior and junior dharma brothers, and then took him to the Buddha-Selection Hall in the meditation grove behind the monastery to take his seat, where nothing happened that night.

The next day Squire Zhao prepared to depart, and though the Abbot urged him to linger, he could not stay. After morning gruel, the Abbot and all the monks escorted him out to the mountain gate. Joining his palms, Squire Zhao said, "Reverend Abbot and venerable masters here, be compassionate in all things. My younger brother Zhishen is a blunt, unpolished, straightforward man; should his etiquette fall short morning or evening, should his words give offense, or should he unwittingly violate the pure rules, I earnestly beg you to overlook it for my humble sake!" The Abbot replied, "Rest easy, Squire; I will slowly teach him to recite sutras, chant mantras, cultivate the Way, and sit in meditation." The Squire said, "In days to come I will surely repay your kindness." Calling Zhishen aside from the crowd beneath a pine tree, he instructed him in a low voice: "Worthy brother, from today onward things cannot be as they were before; watch yourself in everything and never act high and mighty. Should anything go wrong, it will be hard for us to meet again. Take good care of yourself! For seasonal clothes, I will send someone to bring them." Zhishen said, "No need for you to say more, elder brother—I will follow every word." Taking leave of the Abbot and the rest, Squire Zhao entered his sedan chair and went down the mountain homeward with his retainers, the empty chair, and the hampers, while the Abbot led the monks back into the monastery.

Returning to his meditation platform in the Buddha-Selection Hall, Lu Zhishen threw himself down headfirst and went straight to sleep. The two Chan monks on either side shook him awake and said, "This won't do! Since you left the household life, why don't you learn to sit in meditation?" Zhishen barked, "I'm sleeping my own sleep—what business is it of yours?" One Chan monk sighed, "Amitabha—*shanzai*!" Rolling up his sleeves, Zhishen roared, "I eat soft-shelled turtle too—what's this about *shanzai*?" The Chan monk said, "Ah, what suffering!" Zhishen retorted, "A big-bellied fat turtle tastes sweet and delicious—where's the suffering in that?" Neither neighbor said another word and left him to sleep. The next day they went to report Zhishen's rudeness to the Abbot, but the senior monk dissuaded them: "The Abbot said he will achieve an extraordinary fruition later that none of us can match—he's simply shielding him. You have no choice; don't stoop to his level." So the Chan monks went away. Seeing no one dared say a word to him, Zhishen sprawled flat on his back at night in a giant cross-shape across the meditation platform, snoring like rolling thunder; when he got up to relieve himself, he made a tremendous racket and pissed and crapped right behind the Buddha hall, fouling the ground everywhere. An attendant reported to the Abbot, "Zhishen is utterly outrageous, without a shred of monastic decorum—how can a Chan monastery harbor such a man?" The Abbot snapped, "Nonsense! Out of regard for our patron, let him be; he will mend his ways later." After that no one dared speak up.

Before he knew it, Lu Zhishen had stirred up the monastery on Mount Wutai for four or five months. Early winter came on, and having been cooped up in quiet for so long, Zhishen longed for action. On a bright, clear day, putting on his black cloth cassock, tying his raven-blue sash, and changing into monk's shoes, he strode out through the mountain gate. Strolling down to the Half-Mountain Pavilion, he sat on a goose-neck bench and grumbled to himself: "What a damned life! In the old days I had good wine and good meat every single day without fail; now they've made me a monk and starved me half to death! Squire Zhao hasn't sent anyone with goodies for days either—my mouth is so bland a bird could hatch in it! How can I get hold of some wine right now?" Just as he was craving wine, he saw a man in the distance coming up the mountain carrying a pair of covered buckets on a shoulder-pole, holding a wine-ladle in his hand and singing as he climbed:

> "Before Nine-Li Mountain once lay a battlefield,
> Where herdboys still pick up old blades and spears.
> A fair wind ripples the waters of the Wu River,
> Just as Lady Yu parted from the Hegemon-King."

Watching the man carry his buckets up the slope, Lu Zhishen sat in the pavilion and saw him set down his load right at the pavilion to rest. Zhishen called out, "Hey, fellow, what have you got in those buckets?" The man answered, "Good wine." Zhishen asked, "How much a bucket?" The man said, "Monk, are you really joking with me?" Zhishen said, "Who's joking with you?" The man said, "I carry this wine up to sell only to the stoker-Daoists, hall-attendants, sedan-bearers, and workmen at the monastery. The Abbot has issued a strict rule: anyone caught selling wine to the monks is punished by the Abbot, stripped of his capital, and evicted from his house. Since I trade on the monastery's capital and live in the monastery's house, how dare I sell to you?" Zhishen asked, "You really won't sell?" The man said, "Not even if you killed me!" Zhishen said, "I won't kill you—I only want to buy wine from you to drink!" Seeing trouble coming, the man shouldered his buckets and started off. Charging down from the pavilion, Zhishen grabbed the carrying-pole with both hands and kicked him squarely in the crotch. Clutching himself, the man doubled up in a heap on the ground and could not rise for half a day. Carrying both buckets into the pavilion, Zhishen picked up the ladle from the ground, lifted the lids, and ladled the cold wine straight into his mouth. In no time at all he had drunk one whole bucket out of the two. Zhishen said, "Fellow, come to the monastery tomorrow to collect your money!" Having just recovered from the pain, and terrified that the Abbot would find out and ruin his livelihood, the man swallowed his anger without daring to ask for money, divided the remaining wine evenly between the two buckets, grabbed his ladle, and bolted down the mountain as if flying.

Sitting in the pavilion for half the day, Lu Zhishen felt the wine mount to his head. Coming down from the pavilion, he sat for a spell beside a pine root while the wine surged stronger still. Slipping both arms out of his black cassock and tying the sleeves around his waist, he bared the floral tattoos across his back and started up the mountain swinging his arms. Look at him:

> Top-heavy and light-footed, eyes red and face flushed beneath the bright moon; pitching forward and reeling backward, swaying east and west in the cool breeze.
> Staggering and lurching up the mountain like a crane buffeted by wind; rolling and pitching back to the monastery like a tortoise emerging from water.
> His toes once kicked a dragon in the mountain stream; his fists would strike a tiger beneath the peak.
> Pointing at Heaven's Palace, he curses Marshal Tianpeng; kicking open the Netherworld, he seeks to seize the Life-Summoning Judge.
> A bare-chested, naked-bodied Drunken Demon Lord—the arsonist, man-slaying Flowery Monk!

As Lu Zhishen neared the mountain gate, the two gatekeepers spotted him from afar, grabbed their bamboo paddles, hurried to the gate, and barred his way, shouting: "You are a disciple of Buddha—how dare you guzzle yourself dead drunk and come up the mountain! You're not blind—surely you've seen the notice posted in the storehouse: 'Any monk who breaks the precept and drinks wine shall receive forty strokes of the bamboo paddle and be expelled from the monastery; if the gatekeepers allow a drunken monk inside, they too shall receive ten strokes.' Get down the mountain at once and spare yourself a beating!" Being both newly ordained and unchanged in his old ways, Lu Zhishen glared wide-eyed and cursed, "Filthy mother-robbers! If you two want to beat me, I'll fight the pair of you right now!" Seeing things turn ugly, one gatekeeper ran inside like the wind to alert the superintendent, while the other half-heartedly held out his bamboo paddle to block him. Knocking the paddle aside, Zhishen spread his five fingers and slapped the gatekeeper across the face so hard he staggered; as the man tried to steady himself, Zhishen landed another punch that knocked him sprawling beneath the gate groaning in pain. "I'll spare you this time, wretch!" Zhishen roared, lurching inside the monastery. Hearing the gatekeeper's report, the superintendent rallied twenty or thirty workmen, stokers, and sedan-bearers armed with white wooden cudgels, who came rushing out from the west gallery straight into Zhishen. At the sight of them, Zhishen let out a roar like a thunderclap bursting from his lips and charged forward in giant strides. Not realizing at first that he was a former army officer, the men soon saw how ferociously he fought and hastily retreated into the Sutra Hall, slamming shut the lattice doors. Charging up the steps, Zhishen smashed open the lattice doors with a punch and a kick, drove all twenty or thirty men into a dead end, wrested a cudgel from one, and came fighting back out of the Sutra Hall.

The superintendent ran in panic to inform the Abbot. Hearing the news, the Abbot hurried down the gallery with three or five attendants and shouted, "Zhishen, no insolence!" Drunk as he was, Zhishen still recognized the Abbot; tossing aside his cudgel, he stepped forward, made a Buddhist greeting, and pointed down the gallery: "I only drank two bowls of wine and never provoked them, yet they brought a mob to beat me!" The Abbot said, "Out of regard for my face, go straight to sleep—we will talk tomorrow." Lu Zhishen said, "If it weren't for your face, Abbot, I'd beat those bald-headed donkeys of yours to death!" The Abbot bade his attendants help Zhishen to his meditation platform, where he collapsed flat and snored away. Crowding around the Abbot, the office-bearing monks complained: "Days ago we disciples warned Your Reverence—look at him today! How can our monastery tolerate such a wildcat wrecking the pure rules?" The Abbot replied, "Though he makes trouble before our eyes right now, in the end he will attain true fruition. There is nothing for it—out of regard for our patron Squire Zhao, forgive him this once; tomorrow I will summon him and reprimand him myself." Sneering coldly, the monks muttered, "What an undiscriminating Abbot!" and dispersed to rest.

After morning gruel the next day, the Abbot sent an attendant to summon Zhishen from his meditation seat in the monks' hall, where he had not yet risen. When he finally got up, pulled on his cassock, and bolted barefoot out of the hall like a streak of smoke, the startled attendant hurried after him and found him crapping behind the Buddha hall. Stifling a laugh, the attendant waited until he had washed his hands and said, "The Abbot asks to speak with you." Following the attendant to the abbot's quarters, Zhishen heard the Abbot say: "Zhishen, though you come from a soldier's background, since our patron Squire Zhao sponsored your ordination and I laid my hand on your crown to confer the precepts—teaching you not to kill, not to steal, not to commit lust, not to drink wine, and not to lie—those five precepts are the fundamental rule of monastic life, and above all a monk must never drink wine. How could you get dead drunk last night, beat the gatekeeper, smash the vermilion lattice doors of the Sutra Hall, drive away the stoker-Daoists, and roar and bellow like that?" Dropping to his knees, Zhishen said, "I won't dare again!" The Abbot said, "Having left the household life, how could you break the wine precept first of all and violate the pure rules? Were it not out of regard for your patron Squire Zhao, I would certainly expel you from the monastery—never offend again!" Rising with joined palms, Zhishen said, "I dare not, I dare not!" Keeping him in the abbot's quarters, the Abbot had breakfast served to him, reasoned with him gently, gave him a fresh fine-cloth cassock and a new pair of monk's shoes, and sent him back to the monks' hall.

Long ago in the Great Tang, a famous worthy named Zhang Xu composed a "Song of Drunkenness" devoted entirely to wine—truly a fine piece, which runs:

> Golden goblets brim over with the Lord of Joy;
> Lifting them with both hands, the whites of my eyes roll up.
> Craning my neck long like a jade rainbow,
> Swallowing deep, I still lament that rivers and lakes are too narrow.
> In years past, attending a banquet before the Jade Emperor,
> Not two or three guests could match me cup for cup.
> Ripe flat peaches piled like coral;
> Rich jade-nectar poured thick, floating amber.
> Draining hundreds of cups of flowing rosy mist,
> My skin grew glossy and my cheeks flushed faint red.
> Heaven and earth, hearing of my vast capacity,
> Issued an edict granting me three thousand piculs.
> Flying immortals pressed cups upon me past counting;
> Dead drunk, my spirit cleared and sinews felt light.
> The Lord of the East bade me compose new verse;
> Smiling, I pointed at the Three Isles and sang their grace.
> My brush dashed off five hundred words at a stroke,
> Unaware that cap and kerchief had slipped off before the wine-jar.
> Banquet ended, dazed, I forgot the way home;
> Riding a phoenix, I strayed into the Mansion of Cloud-Light.
> Fairy boys helped me down from purple clouds,
> Unable to tell east from west or south from north:
> At one draft, a thousand goblets and a hundred poems,
> While wild cursive script scattered vertical and horizontal strokes!

Whenever one drinks wine, one must never drink to the absolute limit; as the common saying goes, "Wine can accomplish matters, and wine can ruin matters." Even a timid man grows reckless after drinking, to say nothing of a man of fiery spirit!

Now after that drunken brawl, Lu Zhishen dared not step outside the monastery gate for three or four months straight. Then one day in the second month, the weather turned suddenly warm; leaving his cell, he strolled out beyond the mountain gate and stood admiring Mount Wutai for a while. Suddenly he heard a faint *ding-ding dang-dang* ringing carried up from the foot of the mountain on the breeze. Returning to the monks' hall, Zhishen tucked some silver into his breast, walked step by step down the mountain, and passed beneath the archway inscribed "Blessed Land of Wutai"—only to find a bustling market town of five to seven hundred households! Looking around the town, he saw meat stalls, vegetable stalls, taverns, and noodle shops. Zhishen thought to himself: "What a fool I've been! Had I known earlier there was a place like this, instead of robbing that fellow's wine bucket I could have come down and bought my own! My mouth has been watering for days—let's go see what there is to buy and eat!" Tracing the ringing sound, he found blacksmiths forging iron, and next door stood an inn with "Father-and-Son Inn" written over the door.

Walking up to the smithy door, Zhishen saw three men hammering iron and called out, "Hey, master smith, have you any good steel?" Seeing Lu Zhishen's newly shaven cheeks bristling with short, spiky stubble that looked thoroughly intimidating, the smiths were already half afraid of him. Stopping his hammer, the master smith said, "Please sit down, Master—what kind of work do you want forged?" Zhishen said, "I want a Zen staff and a precept-blade forged—have you first-rate iron?" The master smith said, "I happen to have some fine iron right here. How heavy a Zen staff and precept-blade does Master want? Just name it." Zhishen said, "I want a staff weighing a hundred catties." Laughing, the master smith said, "That's too heavy, Master! Not that I couldn't forge it, I only fear how you could ever wield it—even Lord Guan's great halberd weighed only eighty-one catties!" Irritated, Zhishen said, "Am I inferior to Lord Guan? He was only a man too!" The master smith said, "Out of good will, Master, forty or fifty catties would already be plenty heavy." Zhishen said, "Then have it your way—make it eighty-one catties, matching Lord Guan's halberd!" The master smith said, "Master, if it's too thick it won't look right or handle well. Take my advice and let me forge you a sixty-two-catty water-polished Zen staff—if you can't wield it, don't blame me. As for the precept-blade, say no more; I will forge it from the finest iron myself." Zhishen asked, "How many taels of silver for the two weapons?" The master smith said, "No haggling—five taels of silver net." Zhishen said, "I'll give you your five taels of silver, and if you forge them well, there'll be an extra reward!" Taking the silver, the master smith said, "I'll start forging them right away." Zhishen said, "I have some loose silver here—let's go buy a bowl of wine." The master smith replied, "At your pleasure, Master; I have rush work to finish and cannot keep you company."

Leaving the smithy, Zhishen had walked less than twenty or thirty paces when he spotted a wine-pennant hanging beneath the eaves of a shop. Lifting the door-curtain, he went inside, sat down, pounded the table, and shouted, "Bring wine!" The tavern-keeper said, "Forgive me, Master—the house I live in belongs to the monastery, and my capital belongs to the monastery too. The Abbot has issued a strict rule: if any of us sells wine to a monk of the monastery, our capital is confiscated and we are evicted from the house, so you must excuse me." Zhishen said, "Just sell me a little on the sly—I won't say it came from your place." The tavern-keeper said, "Not even on the sly—go drink somewhere else, Master, forgive me!" Having no choice, Zhishen stood up and said, "Once I've had a drink elsewhere, I'll come back and have words with you!" Stepping out the door and walking a few paces, he saw another wine-flag sticking straight out in front of a shop; striding straight inside and sitting down, he shouted, "Landlord, hurry and sell me some wine!" The innkeeper said, "Master, how unreasonable you are! The Abbot has issued a strict rule, as you well know—why come here to ruin our livelihood?" Refusing to budge, Zhishen tried three times and five times, yet the man still would not sell. Realizing he would never get served there, Zhishen rose and walked on, trying three to five more shops without a single one willing to sell. Zhishen thought up a plan: "If I don't think of a trick, how will I ever get any wine?" Far off amid deep apricot blossoms at the very edge of town, a straw whisk hung out from a doorway; walking over to look, he found a small country tavern on the outskirts. Look at it:

> A country wine-shop standing for many years,
> Slanting beside an ancient road bordered with mulberry and hemp.
> White wooden benches set out for guests to sit;
> Low fences woven of brambles and thorns.
> Cracked vats squeeze out yellow-millet wine;
> From the brushwood gate hangs a blue cloth pennant.
> More laughable still is one curious touch:
> A Wine Immortal painted upon a cow-dung-plastered wall!

Lifting the curtain and entering the village shop, Lu Zhishen sat down leaning against a small window and called out, "Landlord, a traveling monk wants to buy a bowl of wine!" Looking him over, the villager asked, "Monk, where do you come from?" Zhishen replied, "I am an itinerant monk wandering on pilgrimage; passing through here, I want to buy a bowl of wine." The villager said, "Monk, if you were one of the masters from the monastery on Mount Wutai, I wouldn't dare sell to you." Zhishen said, "I am not—hurry and bring the wine!" Seeing Lu Zhishen's rough appearance and different accent, the villager asked, "How much wine do you want?" Zhishen said, "Never mind how much—just keep pouring it in big bowls!" After drinking ten or more bowls, Zhishen asked, "What meat have you got? Bring a platter!" The villager said, "We had some beef earlier, but it's all sold out—only vegetables left." Suddenly catching a whiff of savory meat, Zhishen stepped out into the open yard and saw a dog boiling in an earthenware pot beside the wall. Zhishen said, "You've got dog meat right here—why won't you sell me some?" The villager said, "I feared that as a monk you wouldn't eat dog meat, so I didn't offer it." Zhishen said, "Here's my silver!" Handing the silver to the villager, he said, "Sell me half of it right now!" Hurriedly taking out half the cooked dog, the villager pounded some garlic paste and set it before Zhishen. Overjoyed, Zhishen tore off chunks of dog meat with his bare hands, dipped them in the garlic paste, and ate, washing it down with another dozen bowls of wine. Once his appetite was whetted, he kept right on eating and refused to stop; dumbfounded, the villager cried, "Monk, surely that's enough!" Glaring at him, Zhishen roared, "I'm not eating for free—what's it to you?" The villager asked, "How much more do you want?" Zhishen said, "Draw another bucket!" Having no choice, the villager ladled out another bucket, which Zhishen drained in no time; tucking the remaining dog leg inside his robe, he called out as he went out the door, "Keep the extra silver—I'll come back tomorrow to drink more!" Left gaping in terror and speechlessness, the villager watched him stride straight back toward Mount Wutai.

Reaching the Half-Mountain Pavilion, Zhishen sat down a while until the wine surged to his head. Leaping to his feet, he muttered, "I haven't flexed my fists or kicked my legs for ages—my whole body feels stiff! Let me run through a few routines." Stepping out of the pavilion and tucking both sleeves into his sash, he practiced punches and kicks up and down and side to side. Gathering all his strength, he slammed one shoulder against a pavilion pillar—with a loud *gua-la-la* crack, the pillar snapped in two and half the pavilion collapsed! Hearing the crash halfway up the mountain, the two gatekeepers looked down from above and saw Lu Zhishen charging up the slope, staggering with every step. "Disaster!" cried the two gatekeepers. "The other day this beast got drunk, and today he's even worse!" Slamming shut the mountain gate and shooting the bolt, they peered through the crack as Zhishen charged up to the gate. Finding it locked, he pounded on the doors like beating a war-drum while the gatekeepers dared not open. After pounding a while, Zhishen spun around, glared at the Vajra guardian statue on the left, and roared: "You big bird of a fellow, instead of knocking on the gate for me, you shake your fist to scare me? Think I'm afraid of you?" Leaping onto the pedestal, he yanked out the wooden railing as easily as pulling scallions, grabbed a broken beam, and beat the Vajra's legs until clay and paint flaked off in showers. Peering through the crack, the gatekeepers groaned, "Woe is us!" and ran to report to the Abbot. After waiting a while, Zhishen turned around, glared at the Vajra guardian on the right, and roared: "You wretch open your big mouth to laugh at me too?" Leaping onto the right pedestal, he struck the Vajra's feet twice; with an earth-shattering crash, the statue toppled headfirst off its base while Zhishen stood holding his broken beam and roaring with laughter.

The two gatekeepers ran to report to the Abbot, who said, "Do not provoke him—go back to your posts." Meanwhile, the senior monk, superintendent, prior, and all the office-bearing monks crowded into the abbot's quarters to report: "That wildcat is dead drunk again today—he has smashed the Half-Mountain Pavilion and the Vajra guardians at the gate! What is to be done?" The Abbot replied: "From ancient times even the Son of Heaven avoids a drunken man, to say nothing of an old monk like me! As for the broken Vajras, his patron Squire Zhao will come cast new ones; and he shall repair the collapsed pavilion too. Let him be for now." The monks protested, "The Vajra guardians are the lords of the mountain gate—how can they simply be replaced?" The Abbot said, "Never mind breaking the Vajras—even if he smashed the Buddhas of the Three Ages in the main hall, there is nothing to be done; we can only keep out of his way. Didn't you see his violence the other day?" Leaving the abbot's quarters, the monks muttered, "What a muddle-headed Abbot! Gatekeepers, do not open the gate—just listen from inside." Outside, Zhishen bellowed, "Filthy mother-robbing bald donkeys! If you don't let me into the monastery, I'll fetch fire from outside and burn this bird-monastery to the ground!" Hearing his roar, the monks hastily told the gatekeepers to draw back the bolt: "Let that beast in—if we don't open, he really will do it!" Tiptoeing stealthily, the gatekeepers slid back the bolt and darted into their lodge like lightning, while the monks all scattered out of sight.

Pushing both leaves of the mountain gate with all his might, Lu Zhishen tumbled headlong inside and fell flat on his face. Scrambling up and rubbing his head, he charged straight for the monks' hall. Entering the Buddha-Selection Hall where the Chan monks sat in meditation, he lifted the curtain and barged in, startling them all into ducking their heads. Reaching his meditation platform, Zhishen gurgled in his throat and vomited all over the floor. Overcome by the stench, the monks cried, "Amitabha!" and covered their mouths and noses. After vomiting, Zhishen climbed onto his platform, untied his sash, snapped his cassock ties with a *bi-bi bo-bo*, and pulled out that dog leg. "Good, good—my belly's empty anyway!" he said, tearing off chunks to eat. Seeing this, the monks hid their faces behind their sleeves, while his two neighbors edged far away. Seeing them shrink back, Zhishen tore off a piece of dog meat and offered it to the monk on his right: "Have a bite too!" That monk buried his face tight in both sleeves. "Won't eat?" said Zhishen, shoving the meat toward the mouth of the monk on his left; when the monk tried to scramble off the platform, Zhishen seized him by the ear and stuffed the meat into his mouth. When four or five monks from the opposite platform leaped over to intervene, Zhishen dropped the dog meat, raised his fists, and drummed a *ge-cha ge-cha* tattoo across their shaven skulls! Uproar erupted throughout the hall as all the monks grabbed their robes and alms-bowls from the lockers to flee—a commotion known as "rolling up the hall in general flight," which the senior monk was powerless to stop.

Slashing and punching his way out, Zhishen drove most of the meditators out under the galleries. Without consulting the Abbot, the superintendent and prior rallied the office-bearing monks and mustered one or two hundred workmen, stokers, and sedan-bearers; wrapping kerchiefs around their heads and grabbing staves, forks, and cudgels, they charged in a body into the monks' hall. At the sight of them, Zhishen let out a great roar; having no weapon at hand, he dashed before the Buddha altar inside the hall, overturned the offering table, wrenched off two table legs, and came charging out! Look at him:

> Fire blazes in his heart; thunder roars from his lips.
> Rousing an eight- or nine-foot beastly frame, he breathes three thousand fathoms of sky-piercing spirit.
> Unrestrainable his man-slaying gall; wide open glare his sea-churning eyes.
> Charging straight and slashing sideways like a wounded tiger or leopard leaping a cliff; surging forward and billowing back like a spear-struck wolf or jackal bounding across a gorge.

Whirling the two table legs, Lu Zhishen fought his way out; seeing his ferocity, the monks and servants trailed their staves and fell back under the galleries. Sweeping his two table legs low across the ground, Zhishen rolled forward as the monks closed in from both sides; furious, he feinted east and struck west, feinted south and struck north, sparing only those at the far ends. Fighting his way straight to the front of the Dharma Hall, he heard the Abbot shout: "Zhishen, no insolence! Monks, hold your hands!" More than ten men on both sides had been beaten and injured; at the Abbot's arrival, everyone fell back. Seeing the crowd retreat, Zhishen threw down the table legs and cried, "Abbot, stand up for me!" By now he was seven or eight parts sober. The Abbot said, "Zhishen, you have brought endless trouble on me! The first time you got drunk and made a scene, I informed your brother Squire Zhao, who wrote a letter apologizing to the community. Now you have gotten dead drunk again, violated the pure rules, smashed the pavilion, and broken the Vajra guardian—even that might be overlooked, yet you have driven the entire community to roll up the hall and flee, which is no small offense! Mount Wutai here is a sanctuary of the Bodhisattva Manjusri, a pure place of incense and devotion for a thousand years—how can it tolerate such defilement from you? Come stay in my quarters for a few days while I arrange another place for you." Following the Abbot to his quarters, Zhishen watched while the Abbot sent office-bearing monks to keep the meditators from leaving and return them to the monks' hall to resume meditation, while the injured monks went off to nurse their bruises. The Abbot lodged Zhishen in his quarters for the night.

The next day, Elder Zhizhen conferred with the senior monk, gathered some silver to send Zhishen elsewhere, and decided first to inform Squire Zhao. Writing a letter at once, the Abbot dispatched two hall-attendants straight to Squire Zhao's manor to explain the whole affair and await a reply. Reading the letter, Squire Zhao felt deeply displeased and wrote back respectfully to the Abbot: "I will immediately furnish the funds to repair the damaged Vajra and pavilion; as for Zhishen, I leave his disposal entirely to the Abbot." Receiving the reply, the Abbot ordered an attendant to bring out a black cloth cassock, a pair of monk's shoes, and ten taels of white silver, and summoned Zhishen into his room. The Abbot said, "Zhishen, the first time you got dead drunk and disturbed the monks' hall, it could be called an inadvertent offense; this time you got dead drunk again, broke the Vajra, collapsed the pavilion, and drove the community from the Buddha-Selection Hall—your offense is far from light, and you have injured many meditators besides. Renouncing the world here requires a pure sanctuary; behaving as you do is utterly unacceptable. Out of regard for your patron Squire Zhao, I give you this letter to seek a place to settle down—I decidedly cannot keep you here. Last night I looked into your destiny and bestow upon you a four-line gatha that will serve you all your life." Zhishen asked, "Where does Master bid his disciple go to settle down and establish my life? I wish to hear my Master's four-line gatha."

Pointing at Lu Zhishen, Elder Zhizhen spoke the lines directing him to that place. And thereby it was destined that this man would laughingly brandish his Zen staff to battle the heroes under Heaven, and angrily draw his precept-blade to hack down treacherous sons and slanderous ministers—until his fame rang three thousand *li* north of the passes and he attained true fruition in the foremost prefecture south of the Yangtze.
