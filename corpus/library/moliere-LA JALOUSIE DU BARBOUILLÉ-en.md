THE JEALOUSY OF LE BARBOUILLÉ
COMEDY
Molière

After an eighteenth-century manuscript

DRAMATIS PERSONAE

Le Barbouillé, husband to Angélique.

The Doctor.

Angélique, daughter to Gorgibus.

Valère, suitor to Angélique.

Cathau, maidservant to Angélique.

Gorgibus, father to Angélique.

Villebrequin.

Scene I
Le Barbouillé.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I must confess I am the most wretched of men. I have a wife who drives me mad; instead of bringing me comfort and obeying my wishes, she sends me to the devil twenty times a day. Instead of keeping to the house, she is fond of strolling about, of fine living, and of consorting with I know not what sort of company. Ah, poor Barbouillé, how miserable you are! Yet she must be punished. Were I to kill her, the expedient would be worth nothing, for I should be hanged. Were I to have her clapped in prison, the jade would slip out with her skeleton key. What the devil is to be done, then? Ah, here comes Monsieur the Doctor passing this way; I must ask him for some sound advice on how to proceed.

Scene II
The Doctor, Le Barbouillé.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I was just going in search of you to beg a favor of you concerning a matter of great consequence to me.

THE DOCTOR.
You must be exceedingly ill-bred, a great blockhead, and most poorly tutored, my friend, to accost me without doffing your hat, and without observing rationem loci, temporis et personæ. What! To open at once with an ill-digested speech, instead of saying Salve, vel Salvus sis Doctor, Doctorum Eruditissime—pray, whom do you take me for, my friend?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
In faith, pardon me; my wits were all in a sling, and I gave no thought to what I was doing; yet I know full well that you are a gentleman of parts.

THE DOCTOR.
Do you know whence derives the term galant homme?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Whether it comes from Villejuif or Aubervilliers, I care very little.

THE DOCTOR.
Know then that the term galant homme derives from élégant: taking the g and the a from the final syllable, we obtain ga; then taking the l, adding an a and the final two letters, we obtain galant; and then appending homme, we obtain galant homme. Once more, whom do you take me for?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I take you for a Doctor. Come now, let us speak a little of the business I wish to lay before you; you must know—

THE DOCTOR.
Know first that I am not merely a Doctor, but that I am one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, and ten times a Doctor.
First: because just as unity is the base, foundation, and first of all numbers, so too am I the first of all Doctors, the learned of the learned.
Second: because there are two faculties requisite for the perfect knowledge of all things—Sense and Understanding—and since I am all Sense and all Understanding, I am twice a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Agreed, it is just that—

THE DOCTOR.
Third: because the number three is that of perfection according to Aristotle, and since I am perfect, and all my productions likewise, I am three times a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Very well, Monsieur the Doctor—

THE DOCTOR.
Fourth: because Philosophy comprises four parts—Logic, Ethics, Physics, and Metaphysics—and since I possess all four and am thoroughly versed therein, I am four times a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
What the devil, I do not doubt it! Listen to me, then.

THE DOCTOR.
Fifth: because there are five universals—Genus, Species, Difference, Property, and Accident—without knowledge of which it is impossible to construct any sound reasoning; and since I employ them to advantage and know their utility, I am five times a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I shall need a mighty store of patience.

THE DOCTOR.
Sixth: because the number six is the number of labor, and since I labor unceasingly for my renown, I am six times a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Oh, talk as much as you please.

THE DOCTOR.
Seventh: because the number seven is the number of felicity, and since I possess a perfect knowledge of all that can confer happiness, and am indeed happy through my own talents, I feel compelled to say of myself: O ter quatuorque beatum!
Eighth: because the number eight is the number of justice by reason of the equality found within it, and the justice and prudence with which I measure and weigh all my actions render me eight times a Doctor.
Ninth: because there are nine Muses, and I am equally cherished by them all.
Tenth: because just as one cannot pass beyond the number ten without making a repetition of the other numbers, and it is the universal number, so too, when one has found me, one has found the universal Doctor: I contain within myself all other Doctors. Thus you see, by reasons plausible, true, demonstrative, and convincing, that I am one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, and ten times a Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
What the devil is this? I thought to find a learned man who would give me good counsel, and I find a chimney-sweep who, instead of speaking to me, amuses himself with a game of morra! One, two, three, four—ha, ha, ha! Well, that is not the point; I beg you to hear me out, and believe that I am not a man to waste your pains. If you satisfy me in what I require of you, I shall give you whatever you wish—money, if you want it.

THE DOCTOR.
Eh? Money?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Yes, money, and anything else you might ask for.

THE DOCTOR (tucking up his gown behind his backside).
You take me, then, for a man whom money will induce to do anything; for a man wedded to self-interest; for a mercenary soul! Know, my friend, that even were you to give me a purse full of pistoles, and were that purse inside a rich box, that box inside a precious case, that case inside an admirable casket, that casket inside a curious cabinet, that cabinet inside a magnificent chamber, that chamber inside a pleasant apartment, that apartment inside a stately castle, that castle inside an incomparable citadel, that citadel inside a renowned city, that city inside a fertile island, that island inside an opulent province, that province inside a flourishing monarchy, that monarchy within the entire world; and were you to give me the world wherein lay that flourishing monarchy, wherein lay that opulent province, wherein lay that fertile island, wherein lay that renowned city, wherein lay that incomparable citadel, wherein lay that stately castle, wherein lay that pleasant apartment, wherein lay that magnificent chamber, wherein lay that curious cabinet, wherein lay that admirable casket, wherein lay that precious case, wherein lay that rich box in which was enclosed the purse full of pistoles—I should care as little for your money and for you as for that! (Exit.)

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
In faith, I mistook my man: because he is dressed like a physician, I thought one had to speak to him of money; but since he wants none of it, nothing could be easier than to satisfy him. I shall run after him.

Scene III
Angélique, Valère, Cathau.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Monsieur, I assure you that you oblige me greatly by keeping me company from time to time. My husband is so ill-favored, so debauched, such a sot, that it is torture to be with him; I leave you to imagine what satisfaction one can derive from such a lout.

VALÈRE.
Mademoiselle, you do me too much honor in consenting to endure my presence. I promise to contribute with all my power to your diversion; and since you signify that my company is not disagreeable to you, I shall show you by my attentions how much joy I take in the welcome news you impart to me.

CATHAU.
Ah, change your tune—look who is coming, the bringer of ill luck!

Scene IV
Le Barbouillé, Valère, Angélique, Cathau.

VALÈRE.
Mademoiselle, I am in despair at bringing you such distressing tidings, yet you would have learned them soon enough from another; and since your brother is gravely ill—

ANGÉLIQUE.
Monsieur, say no more; I am your humble servant and thank you for the trouble you have taken.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
In faith, without going to the notary, here is the certificate of my cuckoldry! Aha, Madam Jade, I catch you with a man after all the prohibitions I have laid upon you, and you mean to pack me off from Gemini to Capricorn!

ANGÉLIQUE.
Well, must you scold for that? This gentleman has just informed me that my brother is very ill; where is the cause for quarreling?

CATHAU.
Ah, here he is! I wondered how long we should enjoy a little peace.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
In faith, you would corrupt each other, you pair of jades! And you, Cathau, you debauch my wife; since you have been in her service, she is not worth half of what she was.

CATHAU.
Truly, you tell us a fine tale!

ANGÉLIQUE.
Leave that drunkard be; do you not see he is so sodden he knows not what he says?

Scene V
Gorgibus, Villebrequin, Angélique, Cathau, Le Barbouillé.

GORGIBUS.
Is that not my accursed son-in-law quarreling with my daughter once again?

VILLEBREQUIN.
We must discover what the matter is.

GORGIBUS.
What! Forever brawling? Shall you never have peace in your household?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
This hussy calls me a drunkard! Look you, I am sorely tempted to deal you a royal flush across the face in front of your own parents!

GORGIBUS.
Devil take the purse if you had dared do it!

ANGÉLIQUE.
Yet it is always he who begins—

CATHAU.
Cursed be the hour you chose this skinflint—

VILLEBREQUIN.
Come, hold your tongues! Peace!

Scene VI
The Doctor, Villebrequin, Gorgibus, Cathau, Angélique, Le Barbouillé.

THE DOCTOR.
What is this? What disorder, what quarrel, what uproar, what din, what clamor, what dispute, what combustion? What is the matter, gentlemen, what is the matter, what is the matter? Come, come, let us see if there be no means of bringing you to accord; let me be your peacemaker, let me restore union to your house.

GORGIBUS.
It is my son-in-law and my daughter who have been brawling together.

THE DOCTOR.
And what is it about? Let us see; tell me briefly the cause of their dispute.

GORGIBUS.
Monsieur—

THE DOCTOR.
In few words, mind you.

GORGIBUS.
By all means. Put on your cap, then.

THE DOCTOR.
Do you know whence derives the word bonnet?

GORGIBUS.
Not I.

THE DOCTOR.
It comes from Bonum est—"it is good," that is good indeed—because it protects against catarrhs and rheums.

GORGIBUS.
In faith, I did not know that.

THE DOCTOR.
Tell me quickly, then, of this quarrel.

GORGIBUS.
Here is what happened—

THE DOCTOR.
I do not believe you are a man to detain me long, since I entreat you not to; I have pressing affairs calling me to town, yet to restore peace to your family, I am willing to pause a moment.

GORGIBUS.
I shall have done in an instant.

THE DOCTOR.
Be brief, then.

GORGIBUS.
It will be told straightway.

THE DOCTOR.
It must be owned, Monsieur Gorgibus, that it is a fine quality to express things in few words, and that great talkers, instead of gaining a hearing, most often render themselves so importunate that no one listens to them. Virtutem primam esse puta compescere linguam. Yes, the noblest quality of an honest man is to speak little.

GORGIBUS.
You must know, then—

THE DOCTOR.
Socrates most diligently recommended three things to his disciples: restraint in action, sobriety in eating, and saying things in few words. Begin, then, Monsieur Gorgibus.

GORGIBUS.
That is what I mean to do.

THE DOCTOR.
In few words, without ceremony, without indulging in lengthy discourse; cut straight to an apophthegm! Quickly, quickly, Monsieur Gorgibus, make haste, avoid prolixity!

GORGIBUS.
Let me speak, then!

THE DOCTOR.
Monsieur Gorgibus, shake hands! You talk too much; someone else must tell me the cause of their quarrel.

VILLEBREQUIN.
Monsieur the Doctor, you must know that—

THE DOCTOR.
You are an ignoramus, an unlettered fellow, a man devoid of all sound disciplines—an ass, in plain French! What! You begin the narration without a single word of exordium! Someone else must recount this disorder to me. Mademoiselle, tell me a little of the particulars of this commotion.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Do you see that great rascal there, that wine-skin of a husband of mine?

THE DOCTOR.
Gently, if you please; speak with respect of your spouse when you stand before the whiskers of a Doctor such as I.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Ah, truly yes, Doctor! A fig for you and your learning; I am a Doctor myself whenever I please.

THE DOCTOR.
You are a Doctor whenever you please? Methinks you are a merry sort of Doctor! You have the look of one who follows her own caprice: of the parts of speech, you love only the conjunction; of genders, the masculine; of declensions, the genitive; of syntax, mobile cum fixo; and finally, of prosody, you love only the dactyl, quia constat ex una longa et duabus brevibus. Come hither, you; tell me a little what is the cause, the subject of your combustion.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Monsieur the Doctor—

THE DOCTOR.
That is well begun: Monsieur the Doctor. That word Doctor has something sweet to the ear, something full of emphasis—Monsieur the Doctor.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
To my own wish—

THE DOCTOR.
Well said: to my own wish. The wish presupposes desire; desire presupposes means to attain its ends; and the end presupposes an object. Well said: to my own wish.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I am mad with rage!

THE DOCTOR.
Strike out that phrase, I am mad with rage; that is a low and vulgar expression.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Eh, Monsieur the Doctor, listen to me, I pray!

THE DOCTOR.
Audi, quæso, as Cicero would have said.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Oh, in faith, whether it snaps, cracks, or shatters, I care very little—you shall listen to me, or I will smash your doctoral snout! What the devil is the meaning of this?

(Le Barbouillé, Angélique, Gorgibus, Cathau, and Villebrequin all speak at once, each trying to state the cause of the quarrel, while the Doctor also speaks, declaring that peace is a fine thing, creating a confused uproar of voices. Amid the clamor, Le Barbouillé ties a rope around the Doctor's foot and trips him; the Doctor falls flat on his back, and Le Barbouillé drags him off by the rope tied to his foot. As he is dragged away, the Doctor continues speaking and counting off all his arguments on his fingers as though he were not on the ground at all, until he disappears from view.)

GORGIBUS.
Come, my daughter, withdraw to your home and live in harmony with your husband.

VILLEBREQUIN.
Farewell, your servant, and good evening.

Scene VII
Valère, La Vallée. Angélique exits.

VALÈRE.
Monsieur, I am obliged to you for the pains you have taken, and I promise to keep the appointment you have set for me within the hour.

LA VALLÉE.
It cannot be delayed; if you linger a quarter of an hour, the ball will be over in a moment, and you will lose the happiness of seeing her whom you love unless you come at this very instant.

VALÈRE.
Let us go together straightway, then.

Scene VIII
Angélique.

ANGÉLIQUE.
While my husband is away, I shall slip over to a ball given by one of my neighbors. I shall be back before him, for he is off somewhere at the tavern; he will never notice that I went out. That scoundrel leaves me all alone at home as if I were his dog.

Scene IX
Le Barbouillé.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I knew full well I should get the better of that devil of a Doctor and all his confounded learning! Devil take the ignoramus—I laid all his science flat on its back! Still, I must go see whether our good housewife has prepared my supper.

Scene X
Angélique.

ANGÉLIQUE.
How unlucky I am! I went too late; the gathering was over, and I arrived just as everyone was leaving. No matter, it shall be for another time; meanwhile I shall slip indoors as though nothing had happened. But the door is locked! Cathau! Cathau!

Scene XI
Le Barbouillé, at the window; Angélique.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
"Cathau, Cathau!" Well, what has Cathau done, and whence come you, Madam Jade, at this hour and in such weather?

ANGÉLIQUE.
Whence I come? Just open the door for me, and I will tell you afterward.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Oh, in faith, you may go sleep where you came from, or in the street if you prefer it! I open no door to a gadabout like you. How the devil—out all alone at this hour! I know not if it be fancy, but my forehead feels rougher by half already.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Well, what would you say to my being out alone? You scold me when I am in company; how am I to act, then?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
You are to stay indoors, see to supper, look after the household and the children! No more idle chatter—farewell, good night, go to the devil, and leave me in peace.

ANGÉLIQUE.
You will not open to me?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
No, I will not open.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Ah, my poor little husband, I beg you, open to me, my dear little heart!

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Ah, crocodile! Ah, treacherous serpent! You fawn upon me only to betray me.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Open, open, then!

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Farewell. Vade retro, Satanas.

ANGÉLIQUE.
What, you will not open to me at all?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
No.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Have you no pity on your wife who loves you so dearly?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
None; I am inflexible. You have offended me; I am as vindictive as all the devils—which is to say, exceedingly so. I am inexorable.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Do you know that if you drive me to extremity and provoke my anger, I shall do something you will repent of?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
And what will you do, good bitch?

ANGÉLIQUE.
Mark me: if you do not open, I shall kill myself before the door. My parents, who will doubtless come by before retiring to see if we are on good terms, will find me dead, and you will be hanged.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Ha, ha, ha, ha! The fine creature! And which of us two will lose the most by it? Go on, go on, you are not so foolish as to play that trick.

ANGÉLIQUE.
You do not believe it, then? Look, look—here is my knife all ready; if you do not open, this very instant I shall plunge it into my heart.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Take care, it is exceedingly sharp.

ANGÉLIQUE.
You will not open to me, then?

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
I have told you twenty times already that I will not open. Kill yourself, perish, go to the devil—I care not.

ANGÉLIQUE (pretending to stab herself).
Farewell, then! Oh, I am dead!

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Could she truly have been foolish enough to do such a deed? I must go down with the candle and see.

ANGÉLIQUE.
Now to outwit him! If I can slip nimbly into the house while he searches for me, turn about will be fair play.

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Well, did I not know she was not such a fool? She is dead, yet she runs like Pacolet’s wooden horse! In faith, she gave me a genuine fright. She did well to take to her heels, for had I found her alive after giving me such a scare, I would have administered five or six clysters of kicks to her backside to teach her to play the fool. Meanwhile, I shall go to bed. Oh, ho—I believe the wind has blown the door shut. Hey, Cathau! Cathau, open for me!

ANGÉLIQUE.
"Cathau, Cathau!" Well, what has Cathau done, and whence come you, Monsieur Drunkard? Ah, truly, my parents, who will be here in a moment, shall hear the truth of you, you vile wine-skin! You never budge from the tavern, leaving a poor wife with little children—without knowing if they lack for anything—to cool her heels all day long!

LE BARBOUILLÉ.
Open quickly, you she-devil, or I will break your head!

Scene XII
Gorgibus, Villebrequin, Angélique, Le Barbouillé.

GORGIBUS.
What is this? Forever dispute, quarreling, and dissension?

VILLEBREQUIN.
What! Shall you never agree?

ANGÉLIQUE.
Just look at him—here he is dead drunk, coming home at this hour to raise a frightful uproar, and threatening me!

GORGIBUS.
**GORGIBUS.**
Indeed, this is no hour to be coming home. Ought you not, like a good father of a family, to retire early and live in harmony with your wife?

**LE BARBOUILLÉ.**
May the devil take me if I have stepped foot outside the house! Ask these gentlemen down in the pit instead—it is she who has only just returned! Ah, how innocence is oppressed!

**VILLEBREQUIN.**
Come, come now, make your peace; ask her pardon.

**LE BARBOUILLÉ.**
I, ask pardon? I had rather the devil had carried her off! I am in such a rage I am beside myself.

**GORGIBUS.**
Come, my daughter, embrace your husband and be good friends.

### **Scene XIII and Last**

*The Doctor, at the window, in nightcap and undershirt; Le Barbouillé, Villebrequin, Gorgibus, Angélique.*

**THE DOCTOR.**
What now? Forever noise, disorder, dissension, quarrels, debates, disputes, combustions, eternal altercations! What is it? What is the matter, then? There is no getting a moment's rest!

**VILLEBREQUIN.**
It is nothing, Monsieur the Doctor; everyone is in accord.

**THE DOCTOR.**
Speaking of accord, would you like me to read you a chapter of Aristotle, wherein he proves that all the parts of the universe subsist solely through the accord that prevails among them?

**VILLEBREQUIN.**
Is it very long?

**THE DOCTOR.**
No, it is not long; it comprises some sixty or eighty pages.

**VILLEBREQUIN.**
Farewell, good night! We thank you.

**GORGIBUS.**
There is no need of it.

**THE DOCTOR.**
You do not wish it?

**GORGIBUS.**
No.

**THE DOCTOR.**
Farewell, then, since so it is! Good night—in Latin, *bona nox*.

**VILLEBREQUIN.**
Let us go sup together, the rest of us.
